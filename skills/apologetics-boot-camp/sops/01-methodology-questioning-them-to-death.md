# Methodology: Questioning Them to Death

## What it is

Questioning Them to Death is how every SOP is carried out: we lead with questions instead of statements until the false view gives way under its own weight. It is drawn from Gregory Koukl's *Tactics* (2009). Each group's SOP supplies the content; this tab supplies the method.

**The name.** What dies is the error, not the person. Christ questioned his opponents about David's son until "no man was able to answer him a word, neither durst any man from that day forth ask him any more questions" (Matt. 22:41–46). Koukl warns that these tactics are not tricks to embarrass people. They expose bad thinking so the person can be led to the truth.

**The weapon in the war.** Koukl calls his approach diplomacy, not D-Day. Scripture holds both: Paul wages war on arguments (2 Cor. 10:3–5) as an ambassador (2 Cor. 5:20; Eph. 6:20). In these SOPs, questions are the weapon, arguments are the target, and the person is the captive we want freed (2 Tim. 2:25–26). A question that exposes a flaw is a blow aimed at a stronghold, never at the man.

**Why questions.** Scripture shows this method again and again:

- "Counsel in the heart of man is like deep water; but a man of understanding will draw it out" (Prov. 20:5).
- "He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him" (Prov. 18:17).
- Christ asked questions to silence and to teach: "Whether is it easier…?" (Mark 2:9); "The baptism of John, was it from heaven, or of men?" (Luke 20:4); "Whose image and superscription hath it?" (Luke 20:24).
- Paul "reasoned with them out of the scriptures" (Acts 17:2–3).

**The posture.** Be "wise as serpents, and harmless as doves" (Matt. 10:16). Let speech be gracious and seasoned with salt (Col. 4:6). Koukl's rule: if anyone in the conversation gets angry, you lose. Make sure it is your ideas that offend, not you.

**The modest goal.** Aim to put a stone in the person's shoe: one question they cannot stop thinking about. Only the Father draws anyone to Christ (John 6:44). We plant and water; God gives the growth (1 Cor. 3:6–7). Koukl calls this "100% God and 100% man": we answer for faithfulness, God for results.

**Three skills of an ambassador** (Koukl): knowledge (an accurately informed mind), wisdom (an artful method) and character (an attractive manner). The SOPs supply the knowledge; this tab trains the wisdom; Section A of each SOP guards the character.

## The three questions

Never make a statement, at least at first, when a question will do the job. Koukl names this move after the TV detective Columbo, who seems harmless and keeps asking "just one more thing." Use the three questions in order.

| Step | Model question | What it does | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Gather | "What do you mean by that?" | Learns what they believe, so you neither misunderstand nor misrepresent it. Often shows they haven't thought it through. | Feed it back: "Let me see if I understand you…" |
| 2. Burden of proof | "How did you come to that conclusion?" | Whoever makes the claim must defend it. Makes them give reasons, not only opinions. | An alternate explanation is not a refutation. Ask: Is it possible? Plausible? Probable? |
| 3. Lead | Leading questions aimed at a target | Each "yes" walks them one step toward the conclusion. Exposes a flaw without your asserting it. | You need the knowledge first. The SOP's Section C supplies it. |

**Softening step 3.** Start with "Have you ever considered…?" or "Can you clear this up for me?" Ask permission: "Do you mind if I ask a couple of questions about what you've told me?"

**Tell them something they know.** The strongest questions draw out what the person already holds and connect the dots. In the Islamic Dilemma, the Muslim already holds that the Qur'an confirms the Injil and that Jesus was not crucified. The questions only set those two beliefs side by side.

**Narrate the debate.** When someone dodges, step back and describe what happened: "I asked about your claim, and we've moved to another topic. Could we finish the first one?"

## Finding the flaws

Once step 2 has the person's reasons on the table, look for one of these flaws. Then point it out with a question (step 3), not a statement.

| Tactic | What it catches | Sample question | In our SOPs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Suicide** | A claim that fails its own standard | "Is the statement 'there is no truth' true?" | The Qur'an's own test of contradiction (Q 4:82) applied to the Qur'an |
| **Practical Suicide** | A view you can hold but cannot promote without breaking it | "If it's wrong to correct people, why are you correcting me?" | — |
| **Sibling Rivalry** | Two of their claims that cancel each other | "Which of these two is your real concern?" | The Qur'an confirms the Injil; the Qur'an denies the cross the Injil records |
| **Infanticide** | A claim that depends on the very thing it denies | "How can you use that source if it's unreliable?" | Quoting the Gospel to find Muhammad while calling the Gospel corrupt (Leo III's charge to Umar II) |
| **Taking the Roof Off** | A view that, followed consistently, ends in absurdity | "If that's true, wouldn't it also follow that…?" | If Allah made the crowd see a false crucifixion, no eyewitness can be trusted, including those who passed on the Qur'an |
| **Rhodes Scholar** | An appeal to experts without their reasons | "What reasons does that scholar give?" | "Christian scholars say the Bible is corrupted" |
| **Just the Facts** | A claim built on wrong facts | "What exactly is the claim? Is it accurate?" | Manuscripts before Muhammad match the Torah and Gospels we have |

**Taking the Roof Off, step by step.** (1) State their claim and check you have it right. (2) Test-drive it: follow it consistently to see where it leads. (3) If it ends in absurdity, show them and invite them to reconsider where they started. Christ did this with "If Satan cast out Satan…" (Matt. 12:24–28).

**Just the Facts.** Ask first whether the claim looks unlikely on its face, then check it. Precise figures persuade more than general ones.

### Asking the question that exposes the flaw

Each SOP lists the questions that expose its group's flaws, each named by its tactic.

**What makes a good one**

1. **Built from their own commitments,** or, for Just the Facts, from facts anyone can check. Both halves are things they have affirmed in steps 1 and 2, or things their own scripture or publications say.
2. **Every answer costs them.** Map every answer before you ask.
3. **One point, short.** It can be asked in one breath and answered in one word.
4. **Asked last.** Pin down their view and reasons first (steps 1 and 2), so they cannot say "that's not what I believe."

**How to deliver it**

- Ask it once, plainly and gently, then stop talking. Koukl waited in silence rather than let the person off the hook.
- If they dodge, narrate the debate: "I asked whether…; we've moved to another subject. Could you answer that first?"
- Don't answer it for them, and don't gloat. The aim is a stone in the shoe that stays there.
- Then cross to Christ, whether they give the costly answer or dodge (below).

### The bridge to Christ

Exposing the flaw is not the end. Let it raise the question the gospel answers, and cross at once. Philip took the eunuch's question on Isaiah 53, "of whom speaketh the prophet this?", "and began at the same scripture, and preached unto him Jesus" (Acts 8:34–35). Paul began from the Athenians' own altar (Acts 17:23), and Christ turned the Samaritan woman's question about the mountain to himself (John 4:19–26).

- **If they give the costly answer,** ask the bridge question in Section A′ of the SOP. It names the need the flaw has uncovered and offers what Christ gives for it.
- **If they dodge,** don't press the point again. Park it, and give a reason for the hope that is in you (1 Pet. 3:15): "Can I tell you why this matters so much to me?" Then tell the gospel as what Christ has done for you, as Paul did before Agrippa (Acts 26:9–23) and the man born blind before the Pharisees (John 9:25).
- **Fit the telling to the room they give you.** The whole gospel is who Jesus is, our sin, his death for sinners and his rising, and the call to repent and believe (1 Cor. 15:3–4; Mark 1:15). When they ask, or say "go on," tell all of it, as Philip, Peter and Paul did when the eunuch, Cornelius and the jailer opened the way (Acts 8:31–35; 10:33–43; 16:30–32). When they have no time or will for more, tell what this moment opens, and leave the door open: a Gospel of John, an offer to read it together, a time to talk again. At Lystra Paul "keeps to the matter of immediate importance," as Chrysostom says (Acts 14:15–17; *Homilies on Acts* 31); at Athens his speech ends at repentance and the resurrection, and some asked to hear him again (Acts 17:30–32). "All the counsel of God" took him three years (Acts 20:27, 31). Augustine: "the same medicine is not to be administered to all" (*On Instructing Beginners* 15.23). Whatever you say of the gospel must be true and leave no false picture (Gal. 1:8; 2 Cor. 4:2).
- **Ask, don't announce.** "May I show you…?" keeps it a conversation.

## Staying in control and preparing

**When you're outmatched (the hot seat).** Switch from persuading to fact-finding. Ask them to slow down and explain their view and reasons, and take notes. Then close with "Let me think about it." Research it later and come back.

**When they shift the burden onto you.** If you have made no claim, you owe no proof. "I've only asked questions so far. Your view is the one on the table."

**When a question is really a claim.** "Who are you to say?" is a statement in disguise. Ask what they mean until they state it plainly; then it can be tested.

**When someone uses leading questions on you.** You control your side of the conversation. Politely ask them to state their point directly so you can think it over.

**When a steamroller keeps interrupting:**

1. **Stop him.** Raise a hand: "I'm not quite finished." Ask for time to answer one challenge before the next.
2. **Shame him.** Make a direct, courteous request: "Do you want an answer, or an audience?" Use his name.
3. **Leave him.** Give him the last word and walk away. Not everyone is owed an answer (Matt. 7:6; Matt. 21:27). Others listening may still hear.

Each step answers a new interruption: stop him at the first, shame him at the next, leave at the one after that. "Do you want an answer, or an audience?" is fair asked gently, as a question, while he is still talking with you. Said as you walk away, it is a parting jab, so leave with kind words and the door open.

**Why people resist good arguments.** Some resist for reasons of the mind, others because of emotion, prejudice, or plain rebellion (John 3:19). Only God changes that last one.

**Preparing.**

- **Before:** anticipate the objections in each SOP's Section B′ and draft your questions in advance.
- **After:** review each conversation for knowledge, wisdom and character. What would you ask differently?
- **Practice out loud:** role-play with a friend. "The more you sweat in training, the less you bleed in battle."
- **Keep a notebook:** record every new challenge and work out your answer, so no challenge stumps you twice.
- **Leave something behind:** a Gospel of John, offered so Jesus can speak for himself.

## Weighed under Scripture

We take Koukl's method but correct one point where it leans toward neutral ground.

**Where we agree.** Koukl grants that there is no neutral ground on truth and tolerance. With Francis Schaeffer, he holds that every unbeliever must live in God's world and is caught between what he says and what he knows. Scripture says the same: all men know God and suppress that knowledge (Rom. 1:18–21). That is why questions work: they bring up what the person already knows.

**Where we correct him.** Koukl calls the mind, not the Bible, our first line of defense against error. He means first in the order of knowing, not in authority. The mind is God's gift and must be used (Mark 12:30). But it is fallen and must be renewed and ruled by his Word (Rom. 12:2; Prov. 3:5–7). Every thought is taken captive to obey Christ (2 Cor. 10:5), and every teaching is tested "to the law and to the testimony" (Isa. 8:20). So our questions serve Scripture; they do not stand on neutral reason.
