# Eastern Orthodoxy

God's second commandment says of images, "Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them" (Ex. 20:5). In the Greek Old Testament the Orthodox Church reads, that bowing is *proskynesis*. The Seventh Council (Nicaea II, 787) commands that icons be given *proskynesis*, kisses, incense and lights, and says this is veneration, not the worship (*latreia*) due to God alone. This SOP asks one question from the text: does calling the bow veneration instead of worship change what the commandment forbids? Then it points the person to Christ, whom we see by faith in the gospel.

## Short version

**The argument in one sentence.** The commandment forbids bowing to images, using the very word (*proskynesis*) the Orthodox give icons; Scripture shows that neither a lower name for the honor nor an aim at the true God changes that act, so bowing to and kissing icons is what God forbids.

**Before you go: pray.** Pray for them by name, that God would open their eyes and grant repentance, and for clear and gentle words for yourself (Eph. 6:18–20; 2 Tim. 2:25–26; Col. 4:3–4).

**Ask, in order.** Have them read from their own Bible, and wait in silence after the question that exposes the flaw.

1. "When you kiss an icon, what are you doing, and who receives it?"
2. "What's the difference between veneration and worship? Which Greek word does your council use for what icons receive?" (*Proskynesis*.)
3. "Would you read Exodus 20:4–5 with me? In the Septuagint, what's the word for 'bow down'?" (*Proskynēseis*.)
4. "Does the commandment forbid only *latreia*, or the bow too?"
5. "In Exodus 32:5, whom was the feast for? In 32:8, what does God say they did? What does Paul call it in 1 Corinthians 10:7?"
6. "God commanded the brass serpent. Why did Hezekiah break it (2 Kings 18:4)? What does your church offer icons?"
7. **Sibling Rivalry:** "God says of images, 'Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them,' in your Greek, no *proskynesis*. Your council says, give them *proskynesis*. Which should I obey?"

**Avoid:** saying they worship wood and paint (they deny it, and the argument doesn't need it); debating the council's authority, church history or Mary before this lands; mocking the practice. Grant their intent in full and keep to one question: does the name they give the bow change what God forbids? The long version answers every objection.

## Long version

Learn this before the conversation. It explains every step of the short version and shows its support.

**Have open:** Ex. 20:4–5; Lev. 26:1; Ex. 32:4–8; 1 Cor. 10:7; 2 Kings 18:4; Num. 21:8–9; Matt. 4:9–10; Acts 10:25–26; Rev. 19:10; 22:8–9; Rev. 9:20; Gal. 4:8; 1 John 5:20–21.

### A. Foundation

God alone receives the bow of religious devotion, and he will not share it with an image. "I am the LORD: that is my name: and my glory will I not give to another, neither my praise to graven images" (Isa. 42:8). Christ answered the devil's demand for a bow with the law: "Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve" (Matt. 4:10). And John, writing long after the incarnation, ends his letter: "Little children, keep yourselves from idols" (1 John 5:21).

**The name.** "Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them" is the second commandment's own word on images (Ex. 20:5). Both sides receive it as God's word, so it is safe to say to any Orthodox Christian. The Orthodox say their practice agrees with Scripture; this SOP tests that claim with the texts, not with a quarrel over who has authority to read them.

**How we carry ourselves.** Most Orthodox Christians kiss icons out of real love for Christ and his saints, and many came to Orthodoxy hungry for reverence. Their zeal is for God, but not according to knowledge (Rom. 10:2).

- Default to gentleness (2 Tim. 2:24–25; 1 Pet. 3:15).
- Grant what they mean; let the text judge what they do. God judged the golden calf by the act, not by the feast's name (Ex. 32:5, 8).
- Keep sharpness for the teaching that curses all who refuse to bow, and even then keep it true and brief (Titus 1:10–13).

### B. Know them

An Orthodox Christian kisses icons, bows before them, lights candles and burns incense to them, and believes this honor passes to Christ and the saints shown. He says this is veneration, not worship, and that refusing it is a heresy his church condemned in 787.

**Background for beginners.** What a newcomer should know about the Orthodox Church, as it bears on this approach:

- **Who they are.** The churches of the East (Greek, Russian, Antiochian, Serbian and others), in communion with each other, which divided from Rome in 1054.
- **What they believe on authority.** Scripture within Holy Tradition, read through the Fathers and the seven ecumenical councils. Don't argue this here. Their church claims its practice agrees with Scripture, so test the claim at the text.
- **Their Bible.** The Old Testament in the Septuagint, the Greek translation the apostles often quoted. In English, most use the *Orthodox Study Bible*.
- **Practice.** Icons fill the church and the home. Worshippers enter, cross themselves, bow and kiss the icons, and light candles before them. The priest censes the icons during the service. Each year, on the first Sunday of Lent (the Sunday of Orthodoxy), the church celebrates the restoring of icons and repeats its condemnation of those who reject them.
- **What listening costs them.** Nicaea II pronounced anathema, "complete separation from God," on all who refuse to venerate icons, as Schooping shows from its acts (NPNF 2nd series, vol. 14, p. 573). A former Orthodox priest writes that this was not what he was taught in parish or seminary (Schooping, *Disillusioned*, Part I). The person may never have heard it. Say it gently, and only if asked.

**Their distinction.** The Seventh Council decreed of icons "that they should be kissed and that they are an object of veneration and honour (*timitiki proskynisis*), but not of real worship (*latreia*), which is reserved for Him Who is the subject of our faith and is proper for the divine nature." It added incense and lights, as the pious custom of the ancients, and taught, from Basil, that the honor given the image passes to its prototype, the person shown (Nicaea II, Definition, NPNF 2nd series, vol. 14; Basil, *On the Holy Spirit* 18.45). A Greek Orthodox parish sums it up: *latreia* (worship) for God, *hyperdouleia* (high reverence) for Mary, *douleia* (reverence) for the saints, and before an icon an act of *proskynesis*, "a bowing down in reverence" ([St. Andrew GOC](https://saintandrewgoc.org/blog/2019/4/9/the-orthodox-vocabulary-in-worship)).

**Where the words come from.** Augustine set the terms. *Latreia*, he wrote, is the service due to God alone, while the service owed to men is *douleia* (*City of God* 10.1). He did not apply this to images, and he complained of "worshippers of tombs and pictures" in the church of his day (*On the Morals of the Catholic Church* 1.34). John of Damascus (d. 749) gave the distinction its form for icons in his three treatises *On the Divine Images*. Thomas Aquinas went further than the Orthodox: he taught that the image of Christ is to receive the same *latria* as Christ (*Summa Theologiae* III, q. 25, a. 3). There he fails against Ex. 20:5, and the Orthodox themselves don't follow him.

**Their teachers.** Expect John of Damascus, Theodore the Studite and Basil. You may also hear Jonathan Pageau, an Orthodox icon carver widely heard among young converts.

**Terms**

| Term | Meaning to an Orthodox Christian |
| --- | --- |
| Icon (*eikōn*) | A sacred image of Christ, Mary, an angel or a saint, painted by set rules; "written," not painted |
| Veneration (*proskynesis*) | Bowing to, kissing and honoring an icon; distinguished from worship |
| Worship (*latreia*, Latin *latria*) | Adoration due to God alone |
| *Douleia* (*dulia*) | Honor or reverence given to the saints |
| *Hyperdouleia* (*hyperdulia*) | The higher honor given to Mary |
| Prototype | The person an icon shows, to whom its honor is said to pass |
| Theotokos | Mary, "God-bearer" |
| Iconoclast | One who rejects icons; condemned at Nicaea II |

### C. The method: Thou Shalt Not Bow Down

The argument has three premises. The second is the hinge: it tests their distinction. Grant their intent at every step; the argument stands on what is done, not on what they mean.

**Why this approach.** Orthodox Christians are trained to answer "you worship wood" and "icons are idols" with the distinction between veneration and worship, and those talks stall on what they mean. This approach grants what they mean and asks what God forbids. It stays in the commandment and in the Greek Old Testament they themselves read, and the key words are ones their own council uses. It needs no church history and no fight over authority, and it ends with Christ, the true image of God, seen in the gospel (2 Cor. 4:4–6).

#### Premise 1: The commandment forbids the bow itself, not only the worship of the heart

- "Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image, or any likeness of any thing that is in heaven above, or that is in the earth beneath… Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them" (Ex. 20:4–5). The Septuagint has two verbs: *proskynēseis* (bow down) and *latreusēs* (serve). The bow and the service are each forbidden.
- "Neither shall ye set up any image of stone in your land, to bow down unto it" (Lev. 26:1).
- Isaiah mocks the man who "falleth down unto it, and worshippeth it… shall I fall down to the stock of a tree?" (Isa. 44:17, 19). The sin he names is the falling down.
- After the incarnation the charge is the same: men "repented not of the works of their hands, that they should not worship devils, and idols" (Rev. 9:20), where "worship" is *proskyneō*.

#### Premise 2 (the hinge): Calling it veneration (dulia) instead of worship (latria) does not change the act

This is where their defense stands, so it gets four supports.

1. **The commandment forbids both words.** Ex. 20:5 forbids *proskynesis* as well as *latreia*, so a bow that is "only" *proskynesis* is still what it forbids. And Scripture uses the *dulia* word for idolatry: "ye did service unto them which by nature are no gods" (Gal. 4:8), where "did service" is *edouleusate*. Calvin answers the distinction the same way: *dulia* means service, and Scripture forbids serving images (*Institutes* 1.11–12).
2. **Meaning the true God didn't excuse it.** Aaron built the calf and proclaimed, "To morrow is a feast to the LORD" (Ex. 32:5). God's verdict: "they have made them a molten calf, and have worshipped it" (Ex. 32:8), and Paul calls it idolatry (1 Cor. 10:7). Jeroboam's calves too were set up for the God "which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt," and "this thing became a sin" (1 Kings 12:28, 30). Israel had come to call the LORD himself "Baali," my Baal, and God put an end to it: "thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali" (Hos. 2:16). Chemnitz traces all three (*Examination*, Part IV, "Concerning Images," sec. 3).
3. **A holy image God commanded was broken when it was venerated.** God told Moses to make the brass serpent (Num. 21:8–9). It was kept for centuries without sin. But "unto those days the children of Israel did burn incense to it," so Hezekiah "brake in pieces the brasen serpent that Moses had made" and called it Nehushtan, a piece of brass (2 Kings 18:4). Nicaea II commands incense to icons.
4. **Holy creatures refuse the bow, whatever its name.** Cornelius "fell down at his feet, and worshipped him," and Peter said, "Stand up; I myself also am a man" (Acts 10:25–26). John, an apostle, fell to worship before an angel. He would not have meant the worship due to God, yet the angel refused the bow itself: "See thou do it not… worship God" (Rev. 19:10; 22:8–9). Each time the word is *proskyneō*.

**The pagans said the same.** The honor-passes-to-the-prototype defense is older than the church. Augustine answers a pagan who says he worships not the stone but the unseen power it represents (*Expositions of the Psalms*, Ps. 113 [115], sermon 2). Arnobius reports the same plea (*Against the Pagans* 6), and Athanasius records the philosophers' claim that statues are not gods but likenesses through which the gods show themselves (*Against the Heathen*). Chemnitz gathers these and concludes that Rome's cult of images "is also embroidered with similar interpretations" (*Examination*, Part IV, "Concerning Images," sec. 2).

#### Premise 3: Orthodox practice gives icons this bow

- The Seventh Council decrees *proskynesis* and kisses for icons, with incense and lights, and anathematizes those who refuse (Nicaea II, Definition and acclamations, NPNF 2nd series, vol. 14; Schooping, *Disillusioned*, Part II, ch. 2).
- Orthodox teachers say plainly that before an icon the believer makes "an act of proskynesis (the word means a bowing down in reverence)" ([St. Andrew GOC](https://saintandrewgoc.org/blog/2019/4/9/the-orthodox-vocabulary-in-worship)).
- A former Orthodox priest testifies that the church teaches its members "bow to and kiss images with affection," on pain of anathema (Schooping, *Disillusioned*, Part I and Part II, ch. 2).
- Scripture names the kiss as an act of idolatrous homage: "Let the men that sacrifice kiss the calves" (Hos. 13:2); "every mouth which hath not kissed him" (1 Kings 19:18).

#### Conclusion

Orthodox Christians bow to and kiss images, the very act Ex. 20:5 forbids. Calling it veneration doesn't change the act, any more than calling the calf's feast "a feast to the LORD" did. Scripture calls that act idolatry (Ex. 32:8; 1 Cor. 10:7; Rev. 9:20).

**Supporting texts**

- **Images themselves are not the issue:** God commanded the cherubim and the serpent (Ex. 25:18; Num. 21:8). What he forbids is bowing to them (Lev. 26:1; 2 Kings 18:4). Don't argue against all religious art.
- **The image is an idol in their own Bible:** for "graven image" in Ex. 20:4 the Septuagint has *eidōlon*, the word John uses in "keep yourselves from idols" (1 John 5:21), written after "the Son of God is come" (1 John 5:20).
- **Lifeless things:** "They have mouths, but they speak not… They that make them are like unto them" (Ps. 115:5, 8). Schooping argues this, not God's invisibility, is the Bible's main reason for the ban, so the incarnation doesn't lift it (*Disillusioned*, Part II, ch. 3).

#### Question plan

Run it as questions, following Methodology: Questioning Them to Death.

1. **Gather.** "What do you do when you come into church? When you kiss an icon, who receives it? Is that worship?"
2. **Burden of proof.** "Where does Scripture command or show God's people bowing to an image?" "How do you know the honor passes to the person shown?"
3. **Lead.**
   - "Would you read Exodus 20:5 in your Bible? Which Greek word is 'bow down'?"
   - "Does it forbid only *latreia*, or *proskynesis* too?"
   - "At the calf, whom did Aaron say the feast was for? What does God say they did?"
   - "God commanded the serpent. Why did Hezekiah break it?"
   - "When John bowed to the angel, did he mean to worship it as God? Why did the angel stop him?"

**Flaws to watch for** (see the Methodology tab):

- **Sibling Rivalry:** the commandment says "no *proskynesis* to images"; the council says "give *proskynesis* to images." Also: the Septuagint calls the forbidden image an *eidōlon*, while Nicaea II anathematizes "those who call the sacred images idols."
- **Taking the Roof Off:** if a good intent and a lower name make the bow lawful, the calf feast "to the LORD" was lawful too.
- **Just the Facts:** what Nicaea II actually decreed (*proskynesis*, kisses, incense, anathema).

#### Exposing the flaws

Ask these last, after he has read the texts and stated his view. Every answer costs him. Then wait in silence.

| Question | It sets against each other | Every answer costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sibling Rivalry.** **"God says of images, 'Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them,' in your Greek, no *proskynesis*. Your council says, give them *proskynesis*. Which should I obey?"** | Ex. 20:5 (Septuagint) and the Nicaea II definition | *The commandment:* stop bowing to icons. *The council:* the council overrules God's word, which his church denies doing. |
| **Taking the Roof Off.** **"Aaron said the calf's feast was 'to the LORD.' If meaning the true God makes a bow to an image lawful, what was wrong with the calf?"** | Their defense of intent and Ex. 32:5, 8; 1 Cor. 10:7 | *Nothing:* Scripture contradicted. *Something:* intent doesn't make the bow lawful. |
| **Sibling Rivalry.** **"God commanded the serpent, and Hezekiah broke it when Israel burned incense to it. Your church burns incense to icons. What makes the difference?"** | 2 Kings 18:4 and the council's incense | *No difference:* the practice stands under Hezekiah's judgment. *The icon is holier:* the serpent was made at God's own command. |
| **Sibling Rivalry.** **"The angel stopped John's bow and said, 'Worship God.' Would he have welcomed the same bow to his picture?"** | Rev. 22:8–9 and the veneration of icons of angels | *No:* then the bow to his image is refused too. *Yes:* he refused it to his face but takes it at his image. |

### B′. Their answers

Each answer either leaves the commandment's word for bowing untouched, or grants a premise of the argument.

| **"English 'worship' just means honor ('your worship')."** | The question isn't about English. In the Greek they read, the commandment forbids *proskynesis* to images. | Ex. 20:5; Rev. 9:20 |

| **"An idol is an image of a false god; icons show the true one."** | The commandment covers "any likeness of any thing that is in heaven above," and the calf stood for the LORD. | Ex. 20:4; 32:4–5; Deut. 4:15–16 |

**Pitfalls**

- Saying they worship wood. They don't believe they do, and the argument doesn't need it.
- Arguing against all religious art. God commanded images in the tabernacle. Keep to the bow.
- Being drawn into church history or the council's authority. Return to Ex. 20:5.
- Moving to Mary or prayers to saints before this lands. They belong to another conversation.
- Quoting Aquinas against them as if he spoke for them. They don't follow him.

### A′. Turn to Christ

Many Orthodox Christians came looking for reverence and for Christ made near. The gospel gives both: the true image of God, seen by faith, and open access to the Father through him alone.

1. **Bridge from the flaw.** *If he concedes:* "If God forbids the bow to images, where would you look to see Christ? May I show you where Scripture says we see him?" *If he dodges:* park the point and give the reason for your hope (Methodology, "The bridge to Christ").
2. **Show him the true image.** Christ "is the image of the invisible God" (Col. 1:15), and God "hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Cor. 4:6). He is seen in the gospel, by faith: "blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed" (John 20:29).
3. **Show him the one way in.** "There is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Tim. 2:5). He "ever liveth to make intercession" for those who come to God by him (Heb. 7:25), so we may "come boldly unto the throne of grace" (Heb. 4:16).
4. **Show him the gift.** "Being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Rom. 5:1), "not of works, lest any man should boast" (Eph. 2:9).
5. **Ask the question he must answer before God.** "If Christ himself is the image of God, and he invites you to come straight to him, what do you need the board for?"
6. **Keep at it in love.** Leave a Gospel of John. Pray for him by name (Rom. 10:1). Expect many conversations; God gives the growth (1 Cor. 3:6–7).
