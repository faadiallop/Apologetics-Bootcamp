# Jewish people

Isaiah foretold a servant of the LORD who would be pierced for the transgressions of others, cut off from the land of the living, and yet see his days prolonged. This SOP asks a Jewish person, from the Scriptures he already holds, who that servant is, then points him to Yeshua (Jesus).

## Short version

**The argument in one sentence.** Isaiah's servant is innocent, dies for the sins of God's people and then lives and is exalted; he cannot be the sinful nation he dies for, Israel's early interpreters saw the Messiah here, and Yeshua is the one who fits.

**Before you go: pray.** Pray for him by name, that the veil be lifted when he reads (2 Cor. 3:14–16), and for humble, clear words for yourself (Eph. 6:19; Rom. 10:1).

**Ask, in order.** Read Isaiah 52:13–53:12 together, and wait in silence after the question that exposes the flaw.

1. "Who do you think this passage describes? Who taught you that?"
2. "Why did he suffer? What do verses 5 and 6 say?"
3. "Verse 9 says he did no violence and spoke no deceit. Isaiah 1:4 calls Israel 'a sinful nation.' Which one is the servant?"
4. **Sibling Rivalry:** "Verse 8 says he was 'stricken for the transgression of my people.' Just before, God calls Israel 'my people' (52:4–6). If he suffers for Israel, how can he be Israel?"
5. "He is cut off and buried, yet 'he shall prolong his days' (53:8–10). Would you read one Gospel with me to see whether Yeshua fits?"

**Avoid:** calling Isaiah 53 "the forbidden chapter" (see B′); blaming "the Jews" for the cross; starting with the Trinity; contempt for the rabbis. The long version answers every objection.

## Long version

Learn this before the conversation. It explains every step of the short version and shows its support.

**Have open:** Isa. 1:4; 42:1–7; 49:1–7; 52:4–6; 52:13–53:12; Lev. 5:15; 17:11; Dan. 9:26; Eccl. 7:20. New Testament: Luke 24:25–27; Acts 8:30–35; 1 Pet. 2:22–25.

### A. Foundation

The gospel is "to the Jew first" (Rom. 1:16, ESV), and Paul's "heart's desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved" (Rom. 10:1, ESV). Christ opened Moses and the Prophets to show that "the Christ should suffer these things and enter into his glory" (Luke 24:26, ESV; 24:44–47), and Philip, "beginning with this Scripture," Isaiah 53, "told him the good news about Jesus" (Acts 8:35, ESV).

**Why this passage.** The prophets foretold "the sufferings of Christ and the subsequent glories" (1 Pet. 1:10–11, ESV), and the New Testament reads Isaiah 53 of Jesus again and again (Matt. 8:17; Luke 22:37; John 12:38; Acts 8:32–35; 1 Pet. 2:22–25).

**How we carry ourselves.** We come as debtors: "salvation is from the Jews" (John 4:22), and the Christ is theirs "according to the flesh" (Rom. 9:4–5). Gentiles are warned not to boast against the natural branches (Rom. 11:18–20). Christians have wronged Jewish people, often in Jesus' name; own it without excuse.

- Default to gentleness (2 Tim. 2:24–25; 1 Pet. 3:15).
- Expect it to cost him: following Yeshua may divide his family (Matt. 10:34–37). Be patient.
- Keep sharpness for counter-missionaries who lead others astray, and even then keep it true and brief (Titus 1:10–13).

### B. Know them

Jewish people range from Orthodox to Reform to secular. Many have never read Isaiah 53; others have been taught answers to it by counter-missionaries such as Jews for Judaism and Outreach Judaism.

**Background for beginners.** What a newcomer should know about Jewish people, as it bears on this approach:

- **Who they are.** About 15.8 million worldwide, most in Israel and the United States. Jewishness is a people as well as a faith, so a secular Jew with no religious belief is fully Jewish. Many see faith in Yeshua as leaving the Jewish people; Messianic Jews, who believe in him, say it is not.
- **Branches.** Orthodox, Conservative and Reform, and many secular Jews.
- **Scripture.** The Tanakh, the same books as the Christian Old Testament in a different order, read through rabbinic tradition (the Talmud and the commentators). This approach uses the Tanakh alone.
- **The Messiah they expect.** A human king from David's line who will rebuild the Temple, gather the exiles and bring peace to the world (Maimonides, *Laws of Kings* 11–12). So many say Jesus cannot be the Messiah, since the world is not at peace.
- **Atonement today.** With no Temple since AD 70, repentance, prayer and good deeds take the place of sacrifice, above all on Yom Kippur.
- **History.** Christians have persecuted Jews for centuries, often in Jesus' name. Words like "convert," "cross" and "missionary" carry that weight; "Messiah" and "Yeshua" often serve better.

**Final authority.** The Tanakh (Torah, Prophets and Writings), read through rabbinic tradition (the Talmud and the commentators). Many secular Jews hold no religious authority at all.

**Their reading of Isaiah 53.** The prevailing rabbinic view, following Rashi (died 1105), is that the servant is the nation of Israel or its righteous remnant, and that the speakers in 53:1–9 are the kings of the nations, shocked when God vindicates Israel (Outreach Judaism; Jews for Judaism).

**The older reading.** Targum Jonathan renders 52:13 "my servant the Messiah," and the Babylonian Talmud (Sanhedrin 98b) names the Messiah "the leper scholar," citing 53:4. Most early rabbinic sources that discuss the passage apply it to the Messiah or another individual. The collective reading is older than Rashi (a Jew put it to Origen in the 3rd century, *Against Celsus* 1.55), but it became the dominant view through him.

### C. The method: The Suffering Servant

The argument has three premises. Read every verse from the text itself, one premise at a time.

**Why this approach.** Many Jewish people expect a Christian to start with the Trinity or the New Testament, which they don't accept. This approach starts in their own Tanakh, with the passage that most plainly foretells a servant who suffers for others' sins and then lives, a passage their own early tradition read of the Messiah (Targum Jonathan; Sanhedrin 98b). He reads it and says who fits, so the text makes the case, as it did for the Ethiopian official (Acts 8:30–35).

#### Premise 1: The servant is innocent and suffers for the sins of others

- "He was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities" (Isa. 53:5, ESV). "The LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all" (53:6).
- "He had done no violence, and there was no deceit in his mouth" (53:9, ESV).
- His soul "makes an offering for guilt" (53:10), and "he bore the sin of many" (53:12). The guilt offering had to be "without blemish" (Lev. 5:15, 18).

#### Premise 2: The servant is not the nation of Israel

- He is "stricken for the transgression of my people" (53:8). Just before, God calls Israel "my people" (52:4–6; also 51:16).
- Israel is not innocent: "Ah, sinful nation, a people laden with iniquity" (1:4); "all we like sheep have gone astray" (53:6); "there is not a righteous man on earth who does good and never sins" (Eccl. 7:20, ESV).
- The servant's task is "to bring Jacob back to him" and "to raise up the tribes of Jacob" (49:5–6), so he is distinct from the Israel he restores.
- He is cut off, dies and is buried (53:8–9), yet "he shall prolong his days" (53:10): one person's death and life after it.

#### Premise 3: Yeshua is the servant

- He was silent before his accusers (53:7; Mark 14:61; 15:5), counted with criminals (53:12; Mark 15:27), buried by a rich man (53:9; Matt. 27:57–60), and raised (Luke 24:46).
- Israel's early interpreters looked for a person here, the Messiah (Targum Jonathan on 52:13; Sanhedrin 98b on 53:4).

#### Conclusion

The servant is an innocent individual who dies for Israel's sins and lives again. Yeshua did this. Isaiah 53 calls a Jewish reader to him.

#### Ground rules

- **Read the text, not about the text.** Let him read it aloud, from his own translation if he has one.
- **One premise at a time.** Agree that the servant is innocent and suffers for others before asking who he is.
- **Use his words.** Say Yeshua, Messiah and Tanakh.
- **Leave rabbinic sources until he raises them.** Most Jews have not read the Targum or the Talmud.

#### Question plan

1. **Gather.** "Have you read Isaiah 53? Who do you think it describes? Who taught you that?"
2. **Burden of proof.** "What in the text shows the servant is Israel here?" "How do you know the speakers in 53:1–6 are the kings of the nations?"
3. **Lead.**
   - "In 52:4–6, whom does God call 'my people'?"
   - "Was Israel without violence and deceit?"
   - "Can Israel bring Jacob back to God (49:5)?"
   - "Who is buried and then prolongs his days?"
   - "Why did the Targum write 'my servant the Messiah'?"

**Flaws to watch for** (see the Methodology tab):

- **Sibling Rivalry:** "No one can die for another's sin" (Ezek. 18:20) and "innocent Israel suffers for the nations' sins" (the Israel reading itself).
- **Taking the Roof Off:** if Israel's sufferings atoned for the nations, then the nations that persecuted Jews were forgiven by that persecution. Ask gently whether he believes that.
- **Just the Facts:** "*lamo* in 53:8 must be plural." Isaiah 44:15 uses *lamo* of a single idol.

**Pitfalls**

- Calling it the "forbidden chapter."
- Burying a secular Jew in Talmud quotations.
- Saying "you killed Jesus." He laid down his life (John 10:18), and our sins put him there (Isa. 53:5).
- Pressing for a decision when it will cost him his family. Plant, water, and pray.

### A′. Turn to Christ

The passage ends in the servant's victory, and so should the conversation.

1. **Bridge from the flaw.** *If he concedes:* "If the servant isn't Israel, who is he?" It is the eunuch's question (Acts 8:34); answer it as Philip did, beginning at this scripture. *If he dodges:* park the point and give the reason for your hope (Methodology, "The bridge to Christ").
2. **Read the fulfillment.** Read Luke 23–24, or Acts 8:26–39, where a reader of Isaiah 53 meets Yeshua.
3. **Show the guilt offering.** "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29, ESV). By one offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified (Heb. 10:14).
4. **Atonement with no temple.** The Torah ties atonement to blood on the altar (Lev. 17:11), and there has been no altar since AD 70. Yeshua entered the holy places "by means of his own blood, thus securing an eternal redemption" (Heb. 9:12, ESV).
5. **Count the cost together.** Yeshua warned that following him may divide families, and promised a hundredfold now (Matt. 10:34–37; Mark 10:29–30).
6. **Keep at it in love.** Pray for him by name (Rom. 10:1). Leave him a copy of Isaiah 53 and the Gospel of Luke. God gives the growth (1 Cor. 3:6–7).
