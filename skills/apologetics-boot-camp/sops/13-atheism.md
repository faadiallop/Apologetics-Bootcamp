# Atheists

To argue that there is no God, the atheist has to use logic, trust his own mind, expect nature to behave tomorrow as it did today, and call some things truly wrong. His own view of the world cannot account for any of these; only the God of Scripture can. This SOP asks the questions that bring that out, then turns the conversation to the Word who made him.

## Short version

**The argument in one sentence.** To argue against God, the atheist must stand on things only God provides (logic that holds everywhere, a mind that can be trusted, a nature that stays orderly, and right and wrong that truly bind), so he is standing on God's ground to deny him.

**Before you go: pray.** Pray for the person by name, that God would open his eyes and grant repentance, and for clear and bold words for yourself (Eph. 6:18–20; 2 Tim. 2:25–26; Col. 4:3–4).

**Ask, in order.** Wait in silence after the question that exposes the flaw.

1. "When you say you're an atheist, do you mean you believe there is no God, or that you just don't believe in one?" Then: "So what do you think is ultimately real? Just matter, energy and chance?"
2. "How did you come to that conclusion?" and "What would count as evidence for God, for you?"
3. Take one of these, whichever he has leaned on so far:
   - "Do the laws of logic hold everywhere and always? Are they made of matter?"
   - "If your mind came from blind processes that favor survival, not truth, why trust it on the question of God?"
   - "Why do you expect the future to be like the past?"
   - "Was the Holocaust really wrong, or did people just dislike it?"
4. **Infanticide:** "You've used logic, your reason and moral judgment to argue there's no God. Where does your view of the world get them?"
5. "Scripture says the reason we can think at all is that a God who thinks made us, and that we know him and push him away (Rom. 1:19–21). May I tell you what he did about that?"

**Avoid:** debating evolution or the age of the earth; lists of Bible "contradictions"; piling up evidences as if the two of you stood on neutral ground; firing "How do you know?" as a gotcha; calling him a fool or telling him he's "just angry at God"; contempt. The long version answers every objection.

## Long version

Learn this before the conversation. It explains every step of the short version and shows its support.

**Have open:** Ps. 14:1; 19:1–4; Prov. 1:7; 26:4–5; John 1:1–14; Acts 17:22–31; Rom. 1:18–23; 2:14–15; Col. 1:16–17; 2:3; Heb. 1:3; Gen. 8:22.

### A. Foundation

Scripture never treats God's existence as an open question. It begins with him: "In the beginning God" (Gen. 1:1), and "he that cometh to God must believe that he is" (Heb. 11:6). It calls the denial folly: "The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God" (Ps. 14:1; 53:1). Scripture's folly is a matter of the heart, not of intelligence; "the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (Prov. 1:7; Ps. 111:10).

**The atheist already knows God.** This is the ground the whole method stands on.

- "The invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen," so men are "without excuse"; "when they knew God, they glorified him not as God," and they "hold the truth in unrighteousness" (Rom. 1:18–21).
- "The heavens declare the glory of God… There is no speech nor language, where their voice is not heard. Their line is gone out through all the earth" (Ps. 19:1–4). God "left not himself without witness" (Acts 14:17).
- Calvin: a sense of deity is engraved on every mind, so that no one can plead ignorance (*Institutes* 1.3.1). Aquinas: to know in a general and confused way that God exists is implanted in us by nature (*Summa Theologiae* I, q. 2, a. 1, ad 1).

So there is no neutral ground. We don't treat God as a hypothesis the atheist may judge by his own standard. All the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden in Christ (Col. 2:3), and "in him we live, and move, and have our being" (Acts 17:28). The atheist reasons with a mind God made, in a world God orders, by laws that reflect God's own mind. That is the borrowed ground.

**Answering a fool according to his folly.** "Answer not a fool according to his folly, lest thou also be like unto him. Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own conceit" (Prov. 26:4–5). We don't adopt his standard as if it were neutral (verse 4). We do follow his standard to its end, to show it cannot carry the weight he puts on it (verse 5). Bahnsen built his method on these two verses (*Always Ready*), and Paul did the same at Athens, starting from their own altar and poets (Acts 17:22–28).

**How we carry ourselves.** Many atheists are honest, kind and thoughtful. Many were raised in a church and left it hurt; about two in three American "nones" were raised in a religion (Pew Research Center, Dec. 2025).

- Default to gentleness and respect (1 Pet. 3:15; 2 Tim. 2:24–26; Col. 4:6).
- Paul's spirit was provoked by the idols of Athens, yet he reasoned daily and began where his hearers were (Acts 17:16–23).
- "Fool" is God's verdict on the denial, not a name we call the man (Matt. 5:22; Jas. 3:9–10).
- Sharper words belong only to public teachers who lead others astray, and even then they must be true, brief and for the hearers' good (Titus 1:10–13; Matt. 23:13).

### B. Know them

An atheist usually holds that only nature exists and that reason, tested by science, is the only sure guide. He seldom notices that this view cannot account for the reason he trusts. That gap is where the method works.

**Background for beginners.** What a newcomer should know about atheists, as it bears on this approach:

- **How many.** About three in ten American adults are religiously unaffiliated (29% in Pew's 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study). Of these, about one in six calls himself an atheist and one in five an agnostic; most say "nothing in particular" (Pew Research Center, Jan. 2024). So about one American in twenty is a self-described atheist. Most are men, and most are under fifty.
- **Two kinds of atheist claim.** Some say there is no God. Many today say only that they "lack belief" in God, so that the whole burden of proof is on the believer. Antony Flew made this definition popular in "The Presumption of Atheism" (1972). Flew himself later came to believe in a God (*There Is a God*, 2007).
- **The New Atheists.** Richard Dawkins (*The God Delusion*, 2006), Christopher Hitchens (*God Is Not Great*, 2007), Sam Harris and Daniel Dennett shaped how many atheists talk. Online, Matt Dillahunty and *The Atheist Experience* trained many in debate.
- **They know this method.** Many have met presuppositional apologetics online, call it "presup," and have prepared answers (see B′). Some have met it done badly, as a rude loop of "How do you know?"
- **Why they say they don't believe.** The reasons "nones" most often call very important: they can be moral without religion (78%), they question many of religion's teachings (64%), and they don't need religion to be spiritual (54%) (Pew Research Center, Dec. 2025). Morality, then, is often closest to hand.
- **Courtesy.** Ask his story before you argue. Don't assume he is angry, immoral or ignorant. Don't quote Bible verses at him as if he accepted them; ask questions about what he already uses.

**Final authority.** His own reason and experience, usually as tested by science. He does not accept the Bible as authority, so this method works inside his commitments, not inside a scripture.

**View of the Bible.** A human book, often morally troubling to him. He may know it well if he was raised in church.

**God.** Either does not exist, or there is no good reason to think he does. "God" usually means to him a being inside the world, one more thing alongside others, whose existence would be tested like any other claim.

**Morality.** Usually grounded in human well-being or in avoiding harm (Sam Harris, *The Moral Landscape*, 2010). Most atheists hold moral convictions firmly.

**Terms**

| Agnostic | One who says God can't be known, or that he doesn't know | Much of this SOP still serves |

| Presup, TAG | His names for this approach (transcendental argument for God) | Don't use jargon unless he does |

### C. The method: Borrowed Ground

The argument has three premises and one conclusion. Get agreement on each premise before moving to the next.

**Why this approach.** Arguments from evidence (the universe's beginning, design, the resurrection) often stall with an atheist, because he judges each piece by a standard of evidence that has already ruled God out. Bahnsen saw that the real dispute is over the standard itself (*Always Ready*; the Stein debate, opening statement). This approach doesn't ask him to accept anything new. It asks about four things he already uses every time he argues, and shows that his view can't account for them. As in every SOP, we tell him something he already knows (Methodology) and the Christian has nothing of his own to defend first. It is also Scripture's own account of the unbeliever: he knows God and suppresses that knowledge (Rom. 1:18–21).

#### Premise 1: The atheist relies on logic, reason, the order of nature and real moral standards

He relies on four things whenever he argues against God:

1. **Laws of logic** that hold everywhere and always. He calls arguments "fallacious" and expects you to be bound by that.
2. **His own reason,** trusted to find truth.
3. **The order of nature,** expected to hold tomorrow as it did today. Every appeal to science assumes it.
4. **Moral standards** that truly bind. The problem of evil, his favorite argument, calls some things truly evil.

Witnesses: his own words in the conversation (gather them in steps 1 and 2); Gordon Stein, asked in the debate whether there are laws of logic, answered "Absolutely"; and Scripture says the work of the law is written on every heart, so that conscience bears witness (Rom. 2:14–15).

#### Premise 2: A world of only matter, energy and chance can't account for them

Take the four in turn. Each has witnesses from unbelievers' own mouths.

| What he uses | Why his view can't account for it | Witnesses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Logic** | Laws of logic are universal, unchanging and not made of matter. A world of only particular, changing, physical things has no place for them. | Stein granted they are not material and fell back on "conventions" (debate cross-examination); but conventions vary and could have been otherwise, and arguing that logic is a convention already uses logic. Augustine: unchanging truth stands above our changing minds and so cannot be made by them (*On Free Choice of the Will* 2.12–14). |
| **Reason** | If the mind is the product of blind processes that select for survival, nothing guarantees that its beliefs are true. | Darwin: "the horrid doubt" whether the convictions of a mind developed from lower animals are trustworthy (letter to William Graham, July 3, 1881). J. B. S. Haldane: if my thoughts are only the motions of atoms, I have no reason to think them true (*Possible Worlds*, 1927). Alvin Plantinga, *Where the Conflict Really Lies* (2011), ch. 10. |
| **Order of nature** | Nothing in matter and chance promises that the future will be like the past. Arguing that it will, because it always has been, assumes what it sets out to prove. | David Hume, *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, sec. 4. Bertrand Russell's chicken, fed every day until the day its neck is wrung (*The Problems of Philosophy*, 1912, ch. 6). |
| **Morality** | Matter in motion is neither good nor evil. Facts about what is do not yield what ought to be. | Dawkins: the universe has "no evil and no good, nothing but blind, pitiless indifference" (*River Out of Eden*, 1995, ch. 4). Michael Ruse and E. O. Wilson called ethics an illusion our genes give us to make us cooperate ("The Evolution of Ethics," *New Scientist*, 1985). Hume: no "ought" follows from an "is" (*Treatise* 3.1.1). Nietzsche: give up the Christian faith and you lose the right to Christian morality (*Twilight of the Idols*, "Skirmishes," 5). |

#### Premise 3: The God of Scripture accounts for each one

| What he uses | How God accounts for it | Witnesses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Logic** | Logic reflects the mind of the God who is truth, never changes and cannot deny himself. The Word (Greek *Logos*) was in the beginning with God. | Titus 1:2; 2 Tim. 2:13; Mal. 3:6; John 1:1. Augustine: the truth above our minds is God himself (*On Free Choice of the Will* 2.15). |
| **Reason** | Man is made in God's image, to think his thoughts after him, and God gives understanding. | Gen. 1:26–27; Col. 3:10; Prov. 2:6; Job 32:8. |
| **Order of nature** | God has promised it, and Christ holds all things together. | Gen. 8:22; Jer. 33:25; Col. 1:17; Heb. 1:3. |
| **Morality** | God's own holy character is the standard, and he has written it on every heart. | Lev. 19:2; 1 Pet. 1:15–16; Mic. 6:8; Rom. 2:14–15. |

#### Conclusion: the impossibility of the contrary

The atheist's own view can't account for the tools he uses to argue against God; the Christian view accounts for all of them. So the proof of God is, in Bahnsen's words in the Stein debate, "the impossibility of the contrary". Van Til's picture: the unbeliever is like a child who must sit on his father's lap in order to slap his face.

Two cautions. This does not prove itself to an unwilling heart; the natural man does not accept the things of the Spirit, and no one comes unless the Father draws him (1 Cor. 2:14; John 6:44). And it is not a trick; it is a call to stop suppressing what he already knows (Rom. 1:18–21; Acts 17:30).

#### Ground rules

- **Stay on what he uses.** Don't open with evidences, as if the two of you stood on neutral ground; his standard of evidence is the very thing in question.
- **Take one of the four, not all four.** Start with whichever he has leaned on: logic with a debater or philosophy student, morality with most others.
- **Don't let "lack of belief" hide a worldview.** Ask what he thinks is ultimately real. Most atheists turn out to be naturalists.
- **If he says his worldview is off the topic,** show that it is the topic: his charges against God use logic and a moral standard, and the question is where his view gets them (Barker, "we are not debating worldviews today"). Answer the fool so he is not wise in his own conceit (Prov. 26:5), for he knows God already (Rom. 1:19–21).
- **Keep off origins.** The question is not whether evolution happened, but whether, on his view, his mind can be trusted.
- **Ask honestly.** "How do you know?" asked as a loop, without listening, is the arrogance atheists rightly resent (A. E. Wright, "On Presuppositionalism," Secular Web, 2018). Ask to understand (Prov. 18:13), and let him ask you.

#### Question plan

Run the argument as questions, following the three steps of Methodology: Questioning Them to Death. He already uses all four; the questions ask where his view gets them.

1. **Gather.** "What do you mean by 'atheist'?" "What do you think is ultimately real?" "Where do you think right and wrong come from?" Feed it back: "So, if I understand you, everything is matter and energy, and morality is about well-being?"
2. **Burden of proof.** "How did you come to that conclusion?" "What would count as evidence for God?" "Is that standard of evidence itself something the senses can test?"
3. **Lead.** Take one of the four.
   - *Logic:* "Is the law of non-contradiction true in China as well as here? Was it true before people existed? Is it made of matter? Where does it fit in a world that is only matter?"
   - *Reason:* "Did your brain come about by processes aimed at survival? Can a belief be useful for survival and still be false? Then how do you know your beliefs about God are the true ones and not just the useful ones?"
   - *Order of nature:* "Do you expect water to boil at the same temperature tomorrow? Why? How would you show that without assuming it?"
   - *Morality:* "Is it wrong to torture a child for fun, even if everyone voted for it? What makes it wrong, in a world of matter and chance?"

If he says, "I don't know where logic comes from, but I don't need to know," ask: "Then you're trusting something you can't account for. Isn't that what you fault believers for?"

#### Exposing the flaws

Ask these last, after he has affirmed the premise in his own words. Every answer costs him. Then wait in silence.

| Question | It sets against each other | Every answer costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Infanticide.** **"Are the laws of logic made of matter? If not, where do they fit in a world that is only matter?"** | His naturalism and the logic he argues with | *"Conventions":* then a society could vote contradictions true, and his arguments bind no one who doesn't share his convention. *"Descriptions of how the world behaves":* then he needs the order of nature to say they'll hold tomorrow, and they hold of things no one has ever observed. *"In our minds":* then they differ by brain and didn't hold before minds existed. *"Universal within our species"* (Barker): then logic binds no mind but man's, and his charge that God contradicts himself reaches no further than human brains (Ayala). Ask: "Before there were brains, could a thing both be and not be?" *"Brute fact; they just are":* then he has stopped explaining, the thing he faults believers for. |
| **Taking the Roof Off.** **"If your mind came from blind processes that favor survival, not truth, why trust it to tell you there's no God?"** | Naturalism and the trust he puts in his reason | *"True beliefs help survival":* useful and true are not the same (Darwin; Plantinga). *"Science shows reason works":* he uses reason to vouch for reason. *"All views are circular":* then the question is which circle can account for reason (B′). |
| **Just the Facts.** **"Why do you expect the future to be like the past?"** | His appeal to science and his worldview | *"It always has been":* that uses the past to vouch for the past (Hume; Russell's chicken). *"Science":* science depends on it. *"I just assume it":* then his deepest belief is held without evidence, which is his own definition of faith. |
| **Sibling Rivalry.** **"Is the suffering in the world truly evil, or only something we dislike?"** | The problem of evil and his view of morality | *"Truly evil":* on his view there is "no evil and no good" (Dawkins); where does the standard come from? *"Harm is the standard"* (Barker): measuring harm tells how much, not why causing it is wrong. Ask: "Why ought anyone to lessen harm, if he defines morality another way?" The law is written in his heart (Rom. 2:14–15). *"Only disliked":* then his problem of evil has no evil to charge God with. *"Wrong by agreement"* (he may say "intersubjective"): then it turns right when the agreement changes, and a society that agreed on the Holocaust would make it right. Ask: "If the conversation had gone the other way, would it be right?" "Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil" (Exod. 23:2; Isa. 5:20). |

### B′. Their answers

Every common escape either uses the very thing it tries to explain away, or leaves him trusting what he can't account for.

**Pitfalls**

- Running all four premises at once. One, well established, does more.
- Debating evolution, the age of the earth, or Bible "contradictions."
- "How do you know?" asked as a loop, without listening.
- Telling him he is "just angry at God" or doesn't really believe what he says. Scripture says he suppresses the truth; let the questions show it, and don't read his heart for him.
- Treating a hurt ex-Christian like a public debater.
- Stopping at "some god exists." The goal is Christ.

### A′. Turn to Christ

The flaw shows that he stands on God's ground. The Gospel tells him who that God is and how he came looking for him.

1. **Bridge from the flaw.** *If he concedes* ("I can't account for it"): "Scripture says the reason we can think at all is that a God who thinks made us, and that we know him and push him away (Rom. 1:19–21). May I tell you what he did about that?" *If he dodges:* park the point and give the reason for your hope (Methodology, "The bridge to Christ").
2. **The Logos became flesh.** The Word behind all reason "was God… And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us" (John 1:1, 14). Augustine found in the philosophers that the Word was God, but not that the Word became flesh (*Confessions* 7.9). Logic can point to the Word; only the Gospel tells of him coming among us.
3. **Name the real problem, gently.** It is not too little evidence but our sin: "men loved darkness rather than light" (John 3:19; Rom. 3:10–11, 23). This is true of you as well as of him.
4. **The cross and the empty tomb.** "Christ died for our sins… he was buried, and… he rose again the third day" (1 Cor. 15:3–4), before many witnesses (1 Cor. 15:5–8). God "hath given assurance unto all men, in that he hath raised him from the dead" (Acts 17:31).
5. **The call.** God "commandeth all men every where to repent" (Acts 17:30). "Be ye reconciled to God" (2 Cor. 5:20). The restless heart finds its rest in him (Augustine, *Confessions* 1.1; Matt. 11:28).
6. **Keep at it in love.** Leave him a Gospel of John and offer to read it together. Pray for him (Rom. 10:1). Stay his friend after the argument. One plants, another waters, and God gives the growth (1 Cor. 3:6–7; 2 Tim. 2:25).
