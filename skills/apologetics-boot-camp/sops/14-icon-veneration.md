# Icon Veneration

God's commandment says of images, "Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them" (Ex. 20:5). The Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic churches both teach their people to bow before images of Christ, Mary and the saints, to kiss them, and to set lights and incense before them. Both say this is veneration (*proskynesis*, *dulia*), not the worship (*latreia*, *latria*) due to God alone. This SOP asks one question from the text: does calling the bow veneration instead of worship change what the commandment forbids? Then it points the person to Christ, whom we see by faith in the gospel.

## Short version

**The argument in one sentence.** The commandment forbids bowing to images, and their own councils command that bow; Scripture shows that neither a lower name for the honor nor an aim at the true God changes the act, so bowing to and kissing images is what God forbids.

**Before you go: pray.** Pray for them by name, that God would open their eyes and grant repentance, and for clear and gentle words for yourself (Eph. 6:18–20; 2 Tim. 2:25–26; Col. 4:3–4).

**Ask, in order.** Have them read from their own Bible, and wait in silence after the question that exposes the flaw.

1. "When you kiss an icon or kneel before a statue, what are you doing, and who receives it?"
2. "What's the difference between veneration and worship? What does your council say images are to receive?" (Nicaea II: *proskynesis*, a bow, and a kiss. Trent: we kiss them and bow down before them.)
3. "Would you read Exodus 20:4–5 with me? What two things does God forbid doing to them?" (Bowing down, and serving. In the Greek: *proskynēseis* and *latreusēs*.)
4. "Does the commandment forbid only worship, or the bow too?"
5. "In Exodus 32:5, whom was the feast for? In 32:8, what does God say they did? What does Paul call it in 1 Corinthians 10:7?"
6. "God commanded the brass serpent. Why did Hezekiah break it (2 Kings 18:4)? What does your church offer its images?"
7. **Sibling Rivalry:** "God says of images, 'Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them.' Your council says to bow down before them. Which should I obey?"

**Avoid:** saying they worship wood, paint or plaster (they deny it, and the argument doesn't need it); arguing against all religious art; debating the council's authority, church history or Mary before this lands; mocking the practice. Grant their intent in full and keep to one question: does the name they give the bow change what God forbids? The long version answers every objection.

## Long version

Learn this before the conversation. It explains every step of the short version and shows its support.

**Have open:** Ex. 20:4–5; Lev. 26:1; Deut. 4:12, 15–16; Ex. 32:4–8; 1 Cor. 10:7; 2 Kings 18:4; Num. 21:8–9; Matt. 4:9–10; Acts 10:25–26; Rev. 19:10; 22:8–9; Rev. 9:20; Gal. 4:8; 1 John 5:20–21.

### A. Foundation

God alone receives the bow of religious devotion, and he will not share it with an image. "I am the LORD: that is my name: and my glory will I not give to another, neither my praise to graven images" (Isa. 42:8). Christ answered the devil's demand for a bow with the law: "Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve" (Matt. 4:10). And John, writing long after the incarnation, ends his letter: "Little children, keep yourselves from idols" (1 John 5:21).

**The commandment.** "Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them" is the commandment's own word on images (Ex. 20:5). Both churches receive it as God's word, so it is safe to say to any Orthodox Christian or Catholic. Both say their practice agrees with Scripture; this SOP tests that claim with the texts, not with a quarrel over who has authority to read them. The Orthodox, like us, count it the second commandment. Catholics count it as part of the first, so with them say "the commandment about images."

**How we carry ourselves.** Most Orthodox Christians and Catholics honor images out of real love for Christ and his saints, and many came to these churches hungry for reverence. Their zeal is for God, but not according to knowledge (Rom. 10:2).

- Default to gentleness (2 Tim. 2:24–25; 1 Pet. 3:15).
- Grant what they mean; let the text judge what they do. God judged the golden calf by the act, not by the feast's name (Ex. 32:5, 8).
- Keep sharpness for the teaching that curses all who refuse to bow, and even then keep it true and brief (Titus 1:10–13).

### B. Know them

An Orthodox Christian kisses icons, bows before them, and lights candles and burns incense before them. A Catholic kneels and prays before statues and pictures, lights candles before them, and kisses the crucifix. Both believe the honor passes to Christ and the saints shown. Both say this is veneration, not worship, and that their church condemned the refusal of it in 787.

**Background for beginners.** What a newcomer should know about the two churches, as it bears on this approach:

- **Who they are.** The Orthodox are the churches of the East (Greek, Russian, Antiochian, Serbian and others), in communion with each other. Roman Catholics are the churches in communion with the pope. The two divided in 1054. Both receive the Seventh Council (Nicaea II, 787), which commanded the veneration of images.
- **What they believe on authority.** The Orthodox: Scripture within Holy Tradition, read through the Fathers and the seven councils. Catholics: Scripture and Tradition as taught by the pope and bishops. Don't argue this here. Each church claims its practice agrees with Scripture, so test the claim at the text.
- **Their Bibles.** The Orthodox read the Old Testament in the Septuagint, the Greek translation the apostles often quoted; in English most use the *Orthodox Study Bible*. Catholics mostly read the NABRE or the RSV. Both say "bow down" in Exodus 20:5. The older Douay version says "adore."
- **Orthodox practice.** Icons (flat painted images) fill the church and the home. Worshippers enter, cross themselves, bow and kiss the icons, and light candles before them. The priest censes the icons during the service. On the first Sunday of Lent (the Sunday of Orthodoxy) the church celebrates the restoring of icons and repeats its condemnation of those who reject them.
- **Catholic practice.** Churches have statues as well as pictures. People kneel and pray before them and light candles there; statues are carried in processions; and on Good Friday the people come forward to kneel before the cross and kiss it.
- **What listening costs them.** Nicaea II pronounced anathema, "complete separation from God," on all who refuse to venerate images, as Schooping shows from its acts (NPNF, p. 573), and Trent ends its decree on images with an anathema too. A former Orthodox priest writes that this was not what he was taught in parish or seminary (Schooping, *Disillusioned*, Part I). The person may never have heard it. Say it gently, and only if asked.

**Their distinction.** Three official statements carry it.

- **Nicaea II (787), received by both churches.** Images are to be given "due salutation and honourable reverence" (in Greek *aspasmon kai timētikēn proskynēsin*, a kiss and a bow of honor), but not the true worship (*latreia*) that belongs to God's nature alone. Incense and lights may be offered to them, and the honor paid to the image passes on to the one it shows, a line taken from Basil (NPNF, p. 550; Basil, *On the Holy Spirit* 18.45).
- **Trent (1563), for Catholics.** Images are to have "due honor and veneration," not because any power is in them, but because the honor goes to the prototypes, so that through "the images which we kiss, and before which we uncover our head and bow down," Christ is adored and the saints venerated (Session 25, in Chemnitz, *Examination*, Part IV).
- **The Catholic Catechism (1992).** The honor paid to images is "respectful veneration," not the adoration due to God alone, and it passes to the prototype ([Catechism 2132](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/iv_you_shall_not_make_for_yourself_a_graven_image.html)).

A Greek Orthodox parish sums up the vocabulary: *latreia* (worship) for God, *hyperdouleia* (high reverence) for Mary, *douleia* (reverence) for the saints, and before an icon an act of *proskynesis*, "a bowing down in reverence" ([St. Andrew GOC](https://saintandrewgoc.org/blog/2019/4/9/the-orthodox-vocabulary-in-worship)). Catholics use the same three words in Latin: *latria*, *hyperdulia*, *dulia*.

**Where the words come from.** Augustine set the terms. *Latreia*, he wrote, is the service due to God alone, while the service owed to men is *douleia* (*City of God* 10.1). He did not apply this to images, and he complained of "worshippers of tombs and pictures" in the church of his day (*On the Morals of the Catholic Church* 1.34). John of Damascus (d. 749) gave the distinction its form for icons in his three treatises *On the Divine Images*.

**Thomas Aquinas goes further.** He taught that the image of Christ, as his image, is to be adored with the same *latria* as Christ himself (*Summa Theologiae* III, q. 25, a. 3; quoted in Chemnitz, *Examination*, Part IV, "Concerning Images"). There he fails against Ex. 20:5. The Orthodox don't follow him. Catholics honor him as a chief teacher, and the Catechism quotes him on images (2132), so with a Catholic who knows him this shows that "we only venerate" is not the whole of his church's teaching. He will answer that Thomas meant a "relative" worship that passes through the image; that is the prototype defense again, and Premise 2 answers it.

**Their teachers.** Expect John of Damascus, Theodore the Studite and Basil from the Orthodox, and Aquinas, Trent and the Catechism from Catholics. On YouTube you will hear Jonathan Pageau (an Orthodox icon carver), Fr. Josiah Trenham, Jay Dyer and Fr. Stephen De Young for the Orthodox, and Trent Horn, Jimmy Akin, Joe Heschmeyer, Tim Staples and Sam Shamoun for the Catholic side. The Response Bank records their arguments.

### C. The method: Icon Veneration

The argument has three premises. The second is the hinge: it tests their distinction. Grant their intent at every step; the argument stands on what is done, not on what they mean.

**Why this approach.** Orthodox Christians and Catholics are trained to answer "you worship statues" and "icons are idols" with the distinction between veneration and worship, and those talks stall on what they mean. This approach grants what they mean and asks what God forbids. It stays in the commandment, in their own Bibles, and the key words are ones their own councils use. It needs no church history and no fight over authority, and it ends with Christ, the true image of God, seen in the gospel (2 Cor. 4:4–6).

#### Premise 1: The commandment forbids the bow itself, not only the worship of the heart

- "Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image, or any likeness of any thing that is in heaven above, or that is in the earth beneath… Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them" (Ex. 20:4–5). The Septuagint has two verbs: *proskynēseis* (bow down) and *latreusēs* (serve). The bow and the service are each forbidden.
- "Neither shall ye set up any image of stone in your land, to bow down unto it" (Lev. 26:1).
- Isaiah mocks the man who "falleth down unto it, and worshippeth it… shall I fall down to the stock of a tree?" (Isa. 44:17, 19). The sin he names is the falling down.
- After the incarnation the charge is the same: men "repented not of the works of their hands, that they should not worship devils, and idols" (Rev. 9:20), where "worship" is *proskyneō*.

#### Premise 2 (the hinge): Calling it veneration (dulia) instead of worship (latria) does not change the act

This is where their defense stands, so it gets four supports.

1. **The commandment forbids both words.** Ex. 20:5 forbids *proskynesis* as well as *latreia*, so a bow that is "only" *proskynesis* is still what it forbids. And Scripture uses the *dulia* word for idolatry: "ye did service unto them which by nature are no gods" (Gal. 4:8), where "did service" is *edouleusate*. Calvin answers the distinction the same way: *dulia* means service, and Scripture forbids serving images (*Institutes* 1.11.11; 1.12.2).
2. **Meaning the true God didn't excuse it.** Aaron built the calf and proclaimed, "To morrow is a feast to the LORD" (Ex. 32:5). God's verdict: "they have made them a molten calf, and have worshipped it" (Ex. 32:8), and Paul calls it idolatry (1 Cor. 10:7). Jeroboam's calves too were set up for the God "which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt," and "this thing became a sin" (1 Kings 12:28, 30). Israel had come to call the LORD himself "Baali," my Baal, and God put an end to it: "thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali" (Hos. 2:16). Chemnitz traces all three (*Examination*, Part IV, "Concerning Images," sec. 3).
3. **A holy image God commanded was broken when it was venerated.** God told Moses to make the brass serpent (Num. 21:8–9). It was kept for centuries without sin. But "unto those days the children of Israel did burn incense to it," so Hezekiah "brake in pieces the brasen serpent that Moses had made" and called it Nehushtan, a piece of brass (2 Kings 18:4). The text gives the incense as the reason; it does not say they called the serpent a god. Nicaea II allows incense to images.
4. **Holy creatures refuse the bow, whatever its name.** Cornelius "fell down at his feet, and worshipped him," and Peter said, "Stand up; I myself also am a man" (Acts 10:25–26). John fell down to worship "before the feet of the angel which shewed me these things" (Rev. 22:8). He knew it was the angel, and he had already been corrected once (Rev. 19:10), so he had not mistaken it for God. The angel still refused the bow: "See thou do it not… worship God" (Rev. 22:9). Each time the word is *proskyneō*.

**The pagans said the same.** The honor-passes-to-the-prototype defense is older than the church. Augustine answers a pagan who says he worships not the stone but the unseen power it represents (*Expositions of the Psalms*, Ps. 113 [115], sermon 2). Arnobius reports the same plea (*Against the Pagans* 6), and Athanasius records the philosophers' claim that statues are not gods but likenesses through which the gods show themselves (*Against the Heathen*). Chemnitz gathers these and concludes that Rome's cult of images "is also embroidered with similar interpretations" (*Examination*, Part IV, "Concerning Images," sec. 2).

#### Premise 3: Orthodox and Catholic practice gives images this bow

- Nicaea II, received by both churches, decrees a kiss and *proskynesis* for images, with incense and lights, and anathematizes those who refuse (NPNF, pp. 550, 573; Schooping, *Disillusioned*, Part II, ch. 2).
- Trent speaks of "the images which we kiss, and before which we uncover our head and bow down" (Session 25, in Chemnitz, *Examination*, Part IV).
- Their own teachers say so plainly. Before an icon the believer makes "an act of proskynesis (the word means a bowing down in reverence)" ([St. Andrew GOC](https://saintandrewgoc.org/blog/2019/4/9/the-orthodox-vocabulary-in-worship)). An Orthodox priest tells his hearers that the council made icons not merely permitted but necessary, and that all must venerate them (Fr. Spyridon, "Icons: Graven Images or Necessary for Salvation?", [~1:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yt6YHQX0JZs&t=102s)).
- A former Orthodox priest testifies that his church requires its members to "bow to and kiss images with affection" (Schooping, *Disillusioned*, Part I).
- Scripture names the kiss as an act of idolatrous homage: "Let the men that sacrifice kiss the calves" (Hos. 13:2); "every mouth which hath not kissed him" (1 Kings 19:18).

#### Conclusion

Orthodox Christians and Catholics bow to and kiss images, the very act Ex. 20:5 forbids. Calling it veneration doesn't change the act, any more than calling the calf's feast "a feast to the LORD" did. Scripture calls that act idolatry (Ex. 32:8; 1 Cor. 10:7; Rev. 9:20).

**Supporting texts**

- **Images themselves are not the issue:** God commanded the cherubim and the serpent (Ex. 25:18; Num. 21:8). What he forbids is bowing to them (Lev. 26:1; 2 Kings 18:4). Don't argue against all religious art.
- **The image is an idol in their own Bible:** for "graven image" in Ex. 20:4 the Septuagint has *eidōlon*, the word John uses in "keep yourselves from idols" (1 John 5:21), written after "the Son of God is come" (1 John 5:20).
- **God gave words and withheld a form:** "ye heard the voice of the words, but saw no similitude" (Deut. 4:12), and for that reason forbade the graven image (Deut. 4:15–16).
- **Lifeless things:** "They have mouths, but they speak not… They that make them are like unto them" (Ps. 115:5, 8). Schooping argues this, not God's invisibility, is the Bible's main reason for the ban, so the incarnation doesn't lift it (*Disillusioned*, Part II, ch. 3).

#### Question plan

Run it as questions, following Methodology: Questioning Them to Death.

1. **Gather.** "What do you do when you come into church? When you kiss an icon or kneel before a statue, who receives it? Is that worship?"
2. **Burden of proof.** "Where does Scripture command or show God's people bowing to an image?" "How do you know the honor passes to the person shown?"
3. **Lead.**
   - "Would you read Exodus 20:5 in your Bible? What two things does it forbid?"
   - "Does it forbid only worship, or the bow too?"
   - "At the calf, whom did Aaron say the feast was for? What does God say they did?"
   - "God commanded the serpent. Why did Hezekiah break it?"
   - "When John bowed to the angel, did he think it was God? Why did the angel stop him?"

**Flaws to watch for** (see the Methodology tab):

- **Sibling Rivalry:** the commandment says "don't bow down to images"; Nicaea II and Trent say to bow down before them. Also: the Septuagint calls the forbidden image an *eidōlon*, while Nicaea II anathematizes "those who call the sacred images idols." With Catholics: the Catechism says images get veneration and not adoration, while Aquinas gives the image of Christ *latria*.
- **Taking the Roof Off:** if a good intent and a lower name make the bow lawful, the calf feast "to the LORD" was lawful too.
- **Just the Facts:** what Nicaea II and Trent actually decreed (a kiss, a bow, incense, an anathema).

#### Exposing the flaws

Ask these last, after he has read the texts and stated his view. Every answer costs him. Then wait in silence.

| **"The early church had images (catacombs, Dura-Europos)."** | Pictures on a wall are not bowing to them. The early apologists rejected the religious use of images; even Basil and Gregory published Origen's rejection of it. | Schooping, *Disillusioned*, Part II, ch. 2; Origen, in Basil and Gregory, *Philocalia* 19 |
| **"The Seventh Council settled this."** | The council claimed to agree with Scripture. Let's test that at the text it cites. | Ex. 20:5; Acts 17:11 |
| **"You have crosses, nativity sets and Bible films too."** | Having an image isn't the sin; bowing to it is. If we bowed to ours, the same commandment would judge us. | Lev. 26:1; 2 Kings 18:4 |
| **"Aquinas meant a relative worship that passes through the image"** (Catholics). | That is the prototype defense under another name, and it grants more than "we only venerate": the image of Christ is given *latria*. Israel's worship passed through the calf to the LORD, and God called it worship of the calf. | Ex. 32:5, 8; 1 Cor. 10:7; Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* III, q. 25, a. 3 |

**Pitfalls**

- Saying they worship wood or plaster. They don't believe they do, and the argument doesn't need it.
- Arguing against all religious art. God commanded images in the tabernacle. Keep to the bow.
- Being drawn into church history, the canon or the council's authority. Return to Ex. 20:5.
- Moving to Mary, relics or prayers to saints before this lands. They belong to another conversation.
- Quoting Aquinas to an Orthodox Christian as if he spoke for him. He doesn't.
- Saying "the second commandment" to a Catholic without explaining. He numbers it differently.

### A′. Turn to Christ

Many Orthodox Christians and Catholics are looking for reverence and for Christ made near. The gospel gives both: the true image of God, seen by faith, and open access to the Father through him alone.

1. **Bridge from the flaw.** *If he concedes:* "If God forbids the bow to images, where would you look to see Christ? May I show you where Scripture says we see him?" *If he dodges:* park the point and give the reason for your hope (Methodology, "The bridge to Christ").
2. **Show him the true image.** Christ "is the image of the invisible God" (Col. 1:15), and God "hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Cor. 4:6). He is seen in the gospel, by faith: "blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed" (John 20:29).
3. **Show him the one way in.** "There is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Tim. 2:5). He "ever liveth to make intercession" for those who come to God by him (Heb. 7:25), so we may "come boldly unto the throne of grace" (Heb. 4:16).
4. **Show him the gift.** Ask first, "If you stood before God today, what would you trust in?" rather than "Are you saved?", which many Orthodox are taught to hear as arrogant (Response Bank rows 42–43). Then: "He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life" (John 5:24). "Being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Rom. 5:1), "not of works, lest any man should boast" (Eph. 2:9).
5. **Ask the question he must answer before God.** "If Christ himself is the image of God, and he invites you to come straight to him, what do you need the image for?"
6. **Keep at it in love.** Leave a Gospel of John. Pray for him by name (Rom. 10:1). Expect many conversations; God gives the growth (1 Cor. 3:6–7).
