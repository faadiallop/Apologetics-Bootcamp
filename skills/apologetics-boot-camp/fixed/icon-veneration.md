# Those who venerate icons, Eastern Orthodox and Catholic (Icon Veneration)

Status: draft

Source: skill stamp 3ed82382

## opening-beginner

Before we begin, take a moment to pray.

Here is the method. We ask questions instead of making speeches, so that the other person's own Scripture does the work. Christ silenced his opponents with questions, and Greg Koukl's book Tactics trains the same skill.

Here is the argument for Orthodox Christians and Catholics, in one sentence. The commandment forbids bowing to images, and their own councils command that bow; Scripture shows that neither a lower name for the honor nor an aim at the true God changes the act, so bowing to and kissing images is what God forbids.

Why this approach? Orthodox Christians and Catholics are trained to answer "you worship statues" with the difference between veneration and worship, and those talks stall on what they mean. This approach grants what they mean and asks what God forbids. It stays in the commandment, in their own Bibles, with words their own councils use; it needs no church history and no fight over authority; and it ends with Christ, the true image of God, seen in the gospel.

Here is what will happen. You'll choose whether to train first for Orthodox Christians or for Catholics; the argument is the same. You'll learn it in short lessons, then practice in conversations where I play a member of that church, with quick coaching after each. Most of the practice goes to the parts of the method you find hardest, taken in the order Koukl teaches them. Once the method is sure, you'll learn the gospel and how to cross to it; then come real conversations, and at the end you'll teach someone else.

If you know little about these two churches, would you like to begin with who they are, just the facts that bear on this approach?

## background-1

say: Orthodox Christians belong to the churches of the East, Greek, Russian, Antiochian, Serbian and others, all in communion with each other. Roman Catholics are the churches in communion with the pope. The two divided in 1054.

check: Whom are Roman Catholics in communion with?

expect: The pope. A fuller answer may add that the Orthodox churches are in communion with each other, and that the two divided in 1054.

## background-2

say: Both churches receive the Seventh Council, held at Nicaea in 787. That council commanded the veneration of images.

check: Which council, received by both churches, commanded the veneration of images?

expect: The Seventh Council, at Nicaea, in 787 (Nicaea II). The exact year is not required; the gist is enough.

## background-3

say: On authority, the Orthodox hold Scripture within Holy Tradition, read through the Fathers and the seven councils, and Catholics hold Scripture and Tradition as taught by the pope and bishops. Don't argue authority with them. Each church claims its practice agrees with Scripture, so test that claim at the text.

check: Instead of arguing over authority, where do you test their claim?

expect: At the text of Scripture, because each church claims its practice agrees with Scripture.

## background-4

say: The Orthodox read the Old Testament in the Septuagint, the Greek translation the apostles often quoted, and in English most use the Orthodox Study Bible. Catholics mostly read the NABRE or the RSV. These all say "bow down" in Exodus twenty, verse five; the older Douay version says "adore."

check: What do the Bibles they use say in Exodus twenty, verse five?

expect: "Bow down." He may add that the older Douay says "adore."

## background-5

say: Icons, flat painted images, fill an Orthodox church and home. Worshippers come in, cross themselves, bow and kiss the icons, and light candles before them, and the priest censes the icons during the service.

check: What does an Orthodox worshipper do before the icons when he comes into church?

expect: Crosses himself, bows, kisses the icons and lights candles before them. Any two or three of these, in substance, is enough; he may add that the priest censes them.

## background-6

say: On the first Sunday of Lent, the Sunday of Orthodoxy, the Orthodox Church celebrates the restoring of icons. It also repeats its condemnation of those who reject them.

check: What does the Orthodox Church do on the Sunday of Orthodoxy?

expect: Celebrates the restoring of icons and repeats its condemnation of those who reject them. The celebration alone is acceptable if the gist is right.

## background-7

say: Catholic churches have statues as well as pictures. People kneel and pray before them and light candles there, statues are carried in processions, and on Good Friday the people come forward to kneel before the cross and kiss it.

check: What do Catholics do before the cross on Good Friday?

expect: They come forward, kneel before it and kiss it.

## background-8

say: The Seventh Council pronounced anathema, "complete separation from God," on all who refuse to venerate images, and Trent ends its decree on images with an anathema too. A former Orthodox priest writes that he was not taught this in parish or seminary, and the person you talk with may never have heard it. So say it gently, and only if asked.

check: When should you bring up the council's anathema?

expect: Only if asked, and gently, since many members have never heard it.

## foundation-1

say: God alone receives the bow of religious devotion, and he will not share it with an image. Isaiah forty-two, verse eight: "I am the LORD: that is my name: and my glory will I not give to another, neither my praise to graven images."

check: In Isaiah forty-two, verse eight, what does God say he will not give to graven images?

expect: His praise (and his glory he will not give to another). In substance: God will not share the honor due to him with images.

## foundation-2

say: When the devil demanded a bow, Christ answered with the law: "Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve." That's Matthew four, verse ten.

check: How did Christ answer the devil's demand for a bow?

expect: With the law: worship the Lord thy God, and him only serve. The gist, that worship and service belong to God alone, is enough.

## foundation-3

say: John, writing long after the incarnation, ends his first letter: "Little children, keep yourselves from idols." The warning still stands after God became man.

check: How does John end his first letter?

expect: "Little children, keep yourselves from idols," or that gist; he may note it was written after the incarnation.

## foundation-4

say: The commandment's own word on images is "Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them." That's Exodus twenty, verse five. Both churches receive it as God's word, so it is safe to bring to any Orthodox Christian or Catholic.

check: Why is Exodus twenty, verse five safe to bring to an Orthodox Christian or a Catholic?

expect: Both churches receive it as God's word.

## foundation-5

say: Both churches say their practice agrees with Scripture. We test that claim with the texts, not with a quarrel over who has authority to read them.

check: How do we test their claim that the practice agrees with Scripture?

expect: With the texts themselves, not by arguing over who has authority to read them.

## foundation-6

say: The Orthodox, like us, count this the second commandment. Catholics count it as part of the first, so with a Catholic, say "the commandment about images."

check: What should you call this commandment when you talk with a Catholic?

expect: "The commandment about images," because Catholics count it as part of the first commandment, not the second.

## foundation-7

say: Most Orthodox Christians and Catholics honor images out of real love for Christ and his saints, and many came to these churches hungry for reverence. Their zeal is for God, but not according to knowledge, as Romans ten, verse two puts it.

check: Why do most of them honor images?

expect: Out of real love for Christ and his saints; many came hungry for reverence. He may add: zeal for God, but not according to knowledge.

## foundation-8

say: So default to gentleness. Grant what they mean, and let the text judge what they do: God judged the golden calf by the act, not by the name of the feast.

check: By what did God judge the golden calf?

expect: By the act, not by the feast's name (the feast was called "a feast to the LORD").

## foundation-9

say: Don't say they worship wood, paint or plaster. They deny it, and the argument doesn't need it. And never mock the practice.

check: Why not tell them they worship wood or paint?

expect: They deny it, and the argument doesn't need it.

## foundation-10

say: Keep any sharpness for the teaching that curses all who refuse to bow, and even then keep it true and brief. The person in front of you gets gentleness.

check: Where, if anywhere, does sharpness belong?

expect: Only for the teaching that curses all who refuse to bow, and even then true and brief; the person himself gets gentleness.

## know-them-1

say: An Orthodox Christian kisses icons, bows before them, and lights candles and burns incense before them. A Catholic kneels and prays before statues and pictures, lights candles before them, and kisses the crucifix.

check: What does a Catholic do before statues and pictures?

expect: Kneels and prays before them and lights candles; he may add kissing the crucifix.

## know-them-2

say: Both believe the honor passes to Christ and the saints the images show. Both say this is veneration, not worship, and that their church condemned the refusal of it in 787.

check: Where do they believe the honor given to an image goes?

expect: To Christ and the saints the images show, not to the image itself.

## know-them-3

say: They use three Greek words. Latreia, in Latin latria, is worship, due to God alone; douleia, or dulia, is the honor given to the saints; and hyperdouleia, or hyperdulia, is the higher honor given to Mary.

check: Which of these words do they keep for God alone?

expect: Latreia (latria), worship.

## know-them-4

say: Before an icon, an Orthodox Christian makes an act of proskynesis, which a Greek Orthodox parish explains as "a bowing down in reverence." They call this veneration, and set it apart from worship.

check: What does proskynesis mean?

expect: A bowing down in reverence; it is what they call veneration, as distinct from worship.

## know-them-5

say: The prototype is the person an image shows. They say the honor paid to the image passes on to its prototype.

check: What is the prototype?

expect: The person the image shows, to whom its honor is said to pass.

## know-them-6

say: The Seventh Council, received by both churches, says images are to be given "due salutation and honourable reverence," that is, a kiss and a bow of honor, but not the true worship that belongs to God's nature alone. It allows incense and lights to be offered to them, and says the honor passes on to the one the image shows, a line it took from Basil.

check: Besides a kiss and a bow, what does the council allow to be offered to images?

expect: Incense and lights (candles).

## know-them-7

say: The same council pronounced anathema on all who refuse to venerate images. So in their own decree, images receive a kiss, a bow, incense and lights, and refusing is cursed.

check: What did the council pronounce on those who refuse to venerate images?

expect: Anathema, a curse; complete separation from God.

## know-them-8

say: The Council of Trent, in 1563, says images are to have "due honor and veneration," not because any power is in them, but because the honor goes to the ones they show. It speaks of "the images which we kiss, and before which we uncover our head and bow down."

check: What does Trent say Catholics do before images?

expect: Kiss them, uncover their heads and bow down before them.

## know-them-9

say: The Catholic Catechism of 1992 calls the honor paid to images "respectful veneration," not the adoration due to God alone. It too says the honor passes to the prototype.

check: What does the Catechism call the honor paid to images?

expect: "Respectful veneration," not adoration; it passes to the prototype.

## know-them-10

say: Augustine set these terms: latreia is the service due to God alone, and douleia the service owed to men. He did not apply this to images, and he complained of "worshippers of tombs and pictures" in the church of his day. John of Damascus later gave the distinction its form for icons.

check: Did Augustine apply the difference between latreia and douleia to images?

expect: No. He complained of "worshippers of tombs and pictures"; John of Damascus later applied the distinction to icons.

## know-them-11

say: Thomas Aquinas taught that the image of Christ, as his image, is to be adored with the same latria as Christ himself. The Orthodox don't follow him, so never quote him to an Orthodox Christian as if he spoke for him. But with a Catholic who knows him, it shows that "we only venerate" is not the whole of his church's teaching.

check: With whom should you not quote Aquinas as if he spoke for them?

expect: An Orthodox Christian, since the Orthodox don't follow him. With a Catholic who knows him, it shows "we only venerate" is not the whole of Catholic teaching.

## know-them-12

say: The Orthodox will cite John of Damascus, Theodore the Studite and Basil; Catholics, Aquinas, Trent and the Catechism. Online you'll hear Jonathan Pageau, Father Josiah Trenham, Jay Dyer and Father Stephen De Young for the Orthodox, and Trent Horn, Jimmy Akin, Joe Heschmeyer, Tim Staples and Sam Shamoun for the Catholic side.

check: Which teachers is an Orthodox Christian likely to cite?

expect: John of Damascus, Theodore the Studite and Basil; any two is enough.

## know-them-13

say: Learn to say their view so that one of them would answer, "Yes, that's what I believe." For example: we don't worship icons, because worship is for God alone; we venerate them with a bow and a kiss, and the honor passes to the one the image shows.

check: How would you state their view so that one of them would agree?

expect: In their own terms: they venerate and don't worship; worship (latreia) is for God alone; the bow and kiss are veneration; the honor passes to the one shown. It must not call their images idols or say they worship wood or paint.

## argument-1

say: Here is the argument in one sentence. The commandment forbids bowing to images, and their own councils command that bow; Scripture shows that neither a lower name for the honor nor an aim at the true God changes the act, so bowing to and kissing images is what God forbids.

check: What two things does Scripture show do not change the act?

expect: A lower name for the honor (calling it veneration) and an aim at the true God (a good intent).

## argument-2

say: Orthodox Christians and Catholics are trained to answer "you worship statues" and "icons are idols" with the difference between veneration and worship, and those talks stall on what they mean. This approach grants what they mean and asks what God forbids.

check: What does this approach grant them?

expect: What they mean, their intent; it then asks what God forbids.

## argument-3

say: It stays in the commandment, in their own Bibles, and its key words are ones their own councils use. It needs no church history and no fight over authority, and it ends with Christ, the true image of God, seen in the gospel.

check: What does this approach not need?

expect: Church history or a fight over authority.

## argument-4

say: Keep the whole talk to one question: does the name they give the bow change what God forbids? Grant their intent in full; the argument stands on what is done, not on what they mean.

check: What is the one question?

expect: Does the name they give the bow (veneration) change what God forbids?

## argument-5

say: The questions go in order, and he reads from his own Bible. First ask, "When you kiss an icon or kneel before a statue, what are you doing, and who receives it?" Then, "What's the difference between veneration and worship? What does your council say images are to receive?"

check: What do you ask first?

expect: When you kiss an icon or kneel before a statue, what are you doing, and who receives it? The gist is enough.

## argument-6

say: Next, "Would you read Exodus twenty, verses four and five with me? What two things does God forbid doing to them?" Then, "Does the commandment forbid only worship, or the bow too?"

check: After he reads the commandment, what do you ask about worship and the bow?

expect: Does the commandment forbid only worship, or the bow too? (Before that: what two things does God forbid doing to them?)

## argument-7

say: Then the golden calf: "In Exodus thirty-two, verse five, whom was the feast for? In verse eight, what does God say they did? What does Paul call it in First Corinthians ten, verse seven?"

check: Which passage do the questions turn to after the commandment?

expect: The golden calf: whom the feast was for, what God says they did, and what Paul calls it.

## argument-8

say: Then the serpent: "God commanded the brass serpent. Why did Hezekiah break it? What does your church offer its images?"

check: What do you ask after asking why Hezekiah broke the serpent?

expect: What does your church offer its images?

## argument-9

say: Last comes the question that sets the commandment against the council: "God says of images, 'Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them.' Your council says to bow down before them. Which should I obey?" After it, wait in silence.

check: What do you do right after asking that last question?

expect: Wait in silence; don't answer it for him.

## argument-10

say: The argument stands on three premises, and the second is the hinge. The first: the commandment forbids the bow itself, not only the worship of the heart. The second: calling it veneration instead of worship does not change the act; and the third: Orthodox and Catholic practice gives images this bow.

check: Which premise is the hinge?

expect: The second: calling it veneration instead of worship does not change the act.

## argument-11

say: The first premise stands on Exodus twenty, verses four and five: "Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image, or any likeness of any thing that is in heaven above, or that is in the earth beneath… Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them." So God forbids two things toward images: bowing down to them, and serving them.

check: What two things toward images does the commandment forbid?

expect: Bowing down to them and serving them.

## argument-12

say: In the Septuagint, the Greek Old Testament the Orthodox read, the two verbs are proskynēseis, bow down, and latreusēs, serve. The bow and the service are each forbidden.

check: Which of the two Greek verbs means bow down?

expect: Proskynēseis, the proskynesis word; latreusēs is serve.

## argument-13

say: Leviticus twenty-six, verse one says the same: "Neither shall ye set up any image of stone in your land, to bow down unto it." And Isaiah mocks the man who "falleth down unto it, and worshippeth it… shall I fall down to the stock of a tree?" The sin he names is the falling down.

check: In Isaiah forty-four, what sin does the prophet name?

expect: The falling down before the image.

## argument-14

say: After the incarnation the charge is the same. Revelation nine, verse twenty says men "repented not of the works of their hands, that they should not worship devils, and idols," and there the word for worship is proskyneō, the bow.

check: What is the word for worship in Revelation nine, verse twenty?

expect: Proskyneō, the bow, the same word as their veneration; and it is written after the incarnation.

## argument-15

say: The second premise is the hinge: calling it veneration, or dulia, instead of worship, or latria, does not change the act. This is where their defense stands, so it has four supports.

check: Why does the second premise get the most support?

expect: Because their defense, the difference between veneration and worship, stands there.

## argument-16

say: First, the commandment forbids both words. It forbids proskynesis as well as latreia, so a bow that is only proskynesis is still what it forbids.

check: If the bow is only proskynesis, does the commandment allow it?

expect: No; the commandment forbids proskynesis as well as latreia.

## argument-17

say: Scripture even uses the dulia word for idolatry: "ye did service unto them which by nature are no gods," Galatians four, verse eight, where "did service" is edouleusate. Calvin answers their distinction the same way: dulia means service, and Scripture forbids serving images.

check: What does Galatians four, verse eight show about the dulia word?

expect: Scripture uses it for idolatry: serving them which by nature are no gods (edouleusate). Dulia means service, and serving images is forbidden.

## argument-18

say: Second, meaning the true God did not excuse it. Aaron built the calf and proclaimed, "To morrow is a feast to the LORD," yet God's verdict was, "they have made them a molten calf, and have worshipped it." Paul calls it idolatry in First Corinthians ten, verse seven.

check: Though the feast was "to the LORD," what did God say Israel did?

expect: They made a molten calf and worshipped it; Paul calls it idolatry.

## argument-19

say: Jeroboam's calves too were set up for the God "which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt," and "this thing became a sin." Israel had even come to call the LORD himself Baali, my Baal, and God put an end to it: "thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali."

check: For which God were Jeroboam's calves set up?

expect: The true God, the one who brought Israel out of Egypt; yet it became a sin.

## argument-20

say: Third, a holy image God commanded was broken when it was venerated. God told Moses to make the brass serpent, and it was kept for centuries without sin. But "unto those days the children of Israel did burn incense to it," so Hezekiah "brake in pieces the brasen serpent that Moses had made."

check: Why did Hezekiah break the serpent?

expect: Because Israel burned incense to it.

## argument-21

say: The text gives the incense as the reason; it does not say they called the serpent a god. Hezekiah called it Nehushtan, a piece of brass. And their own council allows incense to images.

check: What does their council allow that Israel did to the serpent?

expect: Incense offered to images.

## argument-22

say: So ask, "God commanded the serpent, and Hezekiah broke it when Israel burned incense to it. Your church sets incense and candles before images. What makes the difference?" If he says his image is holier, the serpent was made at God's own command; if he says Israel thought it a god, the text doesn't say so, it names the incense.

check: If he says Israel broke the serpent because they thought it a god, what do you point to?

expect: The text doesn't say they thought it a god; it names the incense as the reason.

## argument-23

say: Fourth, holy creatures refuse the bow, whatever its name. Cornelius "fell down at his feet, and worshipped him," and Peter said, "Stand up; I myself also am a man."

check: What did Peter do when Cornelius bowed to him?

expect: He refused it: stand up; I myself also am a man.

## argument-24

say: John fell down to worship "before the feet of the angel which shewed me these things." He knew it was the angel, and had been corrected once before, yet the angel still refused the bow: "See thou do it not… worship God." Each time, with Peter and with the angel, the word is proskyneō.

check: Did John mistake the angel for God?

expect: No; he knew it was the angel and had been corrected before, yet the angel still refused the bow.

## argument-25

say: So ask, "The angel stopped John's bow and said, 'Worship God.' Would he have welcomed the same bow to his picture?" If he says no, then the bow to the angel's image is refused too; if yes, the angel refused it to his face but takes it at his image.

check: If he answers no, what follows?

expect: The bow to the angel's image is refused too.

## argument-26

say: The defense that the honor passes on to the one shown is older than the church. Augustine answered a pagan who said he worshipped not the stone but the unseen power it stood for, and Arnobius and Athanasius record the same plea. Chemnitz gathers these and concludes that Rome's cult of images "is also embroidered with similar interpretations."

check: Who made this defense before the church did?

expect: The pagans: they said they worshipped not the stone but the unseen power it represented.

## argument-27

say: The third premise: Orthodox and Catholic practice gives images this bow. The Seventh Council decrees a kiss and proskynesis for images, with incense and lights, and anathematizes those who refuse; Trent speaks of "the images which we kiss, and before which we uncover our head and bow down."

check: What does the Seventh Council decree for images?

expect: A kiss and proskynesis (a bow), with incense and lights, and anathema on those who refuse.

## argument-28

say: Their own teachers say it plainly. A Greek Orthodox parish calls the act before an icon "a bowing down in reverence," an Orthodox priest tells his hearers that the council made icons necessary and all must venerate them, and a former Orthodox priest says his church requires its members to "bow to and kiss images with affection."

check: What does the former Orthodox priest say his church requires?

expect: That its members bow to and kiss images with affection.

## argument-29

say: Scripture names the kiss as an act of idolatrous homage: "Let the men that sacrifice kiss the calves," Hosea thirteen, verse two, and "every mouth which hath not kissed him," First Kings nineteen, verse eighteen.

check: Besides the bow, what act does Scripture name as idolatrous homage?

expect: The kiss.

## argument-30

say: So the conclusion: Orthodox Christians and Catholics bow to and kiss images, the very act Exodus twenty, verse five forbids. Calling it veneration does not change the act, any more than calling the calf's feast "a feast to the LORD" did, and Scripture calls that act idolatry.

check: What does Scripture call the act of bowing to an image?

expect: Idolatry; the name veneration doesn't change the act.

## argument-31

say: Images themselves are not the issue: God commanded the cherubim and the serpent. What he forbids is bowing to them, so never argue against all religious art.

check: What does God forbid: making images, or bowing to them?

expect: Bowing to them; he commanded some images himself, so don't argue against all religious art.

## argument-32

say: In their own Greek Bible, the word for "graven image" in Exodus twenty, verse four is eidōlon, the word John uses in "keep yourselves from idols." And John wrote that after "the Son of God is come."

check: What Greek word does their Bible use for the graven image?

expect: Eidōlon, idol, the word John uses in "keep yourselves from idols," written after the incarnation.

## argument-33

say: At Sinai God gave words and withheld a form: "ye heard the voice of the words, but saw no similitude," Deuteronomy four, verse twelve. For that reason he forbade the graven image.

check: Why, according to Deuteronomy four, did God forbid the graven image?

expect: Because Israel heard his words but saw no form (no similitude).

## argument-34

say: Psalm one hundred fifteen says of images, "They have mouths, but they speak not… They that make them are like unto them." A former Orthodox priest, Joshua Schooping, argues that this lifelessness, not God's invisibility, is the Bible's main reason for the ban, so the incarnation does not lift it.

check: Why, on this reading, does the incarnation not lift the ban?

expect: Because the Bible's main reason for the ban is that images are lifeless, not that God is invisible.

## tactic-gather

say: The first tactic is gathering: you ask "What do you mean by that?" before you answer anything. You use it when he uses a loaded word or states his view loosely, as in "we venerate, we don't worship." With this group ask, "What do you do when you come into church? When you kiss an icon or kneel before a statue, who receives it? Is that worship?"

check: When does a member's line call for gathering?

expect: When he uses a loaded word or states his view loosely; then you ask what he means before answering.

## tactic-burden-of-proof

say: The second tactic is the burden of proof: "How did you come to that conclusion?" Whenever he makes a claim, he owes the reasons, not you. So when he says the honor passes to the person shown, ask, "How do you know the honor passes to the person shown?" or "Where does Scripture command or show God's people bowing to an image?"

check: When he makes a claim, who owes the reasons?

expect: He does, the one who made the claim; ask him how he came to it.

## tactic-rescues

say: Two rescues keep you from carrying a load that isn't yours. If he shifts the burden onto you when you've only asked questions, say, "I've only asked questions so far. Your view is the one on the table." If he outclasses you, as a priest with the Septuagint, the Fathers and the councils may, switch to fact-finding: ask him to explain his view and reasons, take notes, and close with "Let me think about it."

check: What do you do when a member outclasses you?

expect: Switch from persuading to fact-finding: ask him to explain his view and reasons, take notes, and say "Let me think about it." No bluffing and no giving up.

## tactic-staying-on-topic

say: Staying on topic keeps you on the one question: does the name of the bow change what Exodus twenty, verse five forbids? When he raises a side issue, let a passing jab pass, park a real question in a sentence, or narrate if he keeps drifting, and return by name. If a priest asks who you are to overturn the Seventh Council, return with, "Your council says it agrees with Scripture. Shall we test that at the text it cites?"

check: What are the three ways back to the topic?

expect: Let it pass, park it, or narrate; then return to the one question by name.

## tactic-steamroller

say: The steamroller is a member who interrupts or piles on, as a convert apologist may, firing the canon, relics and Nestorianism at you without a pause. At his first interruption, stop him: "May I answer your first point before we go to the next?" At the next, ask gently, "Do you want an answer, or an audience?" and at the one after that, give him the last word and leave kindly, with no jab.

check: What do you do at his third interruption?

expect: Give him the last word and leave kindly, with no jab on the way out.

## tactic-lead

say: Leading is the third step: once he has given his view and his reasons, you make the point with questions, not statements, each one walking him a step on. Have him read Exodus twenty, verse five and ask, "What two things does it forbid?" then "Does it forbid only worship, or the bow too?" and go on to the calf, the serpent and the angel, until he says in his own words what the commandment forbids.

check: When do you begin leading with questions?

expect: After he has stated his view and his reasons.

## tactic-turnabout

say: The turnabout answers a member who leads you with questions, or makes a claim in the form of a question. Don't answer the trap; ask him to state his point. If he asks whether you'd trample a picture of Christ, ask, "Are you saying that refusing to insult a picture is the same as bowing to it?"

check: When a member leads you with a question toward a point he hasn't stated, what do you do?

expect: Ask him to state his point plainly instead of answering the trap.

## tactic-sibling-rivalry

say: Sibling Rivalry shows, with a question and never a charge, that two things he has affirmed cancel each other, here the commandment and his council. Ask, "God says of images, 'Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them.' Your council says to bow down before them. Which should I obey?" If he says the commandment, he should stop bowing to images; if the council, it overrules God's word, which his church denies doing.

check: If he answers that he obeys the council, what does that cost him?

expect: It sets the council over God's word, which his church denies doing.

## tactic-taking-the-roof-off

say: Taking the Roof Off follows his view consistently until it ends in absurdity, and it's called for when he defends the bow by his intent, that it is meant for God. Ask, "Aaron said the calf's feast was 'to the LORD.' If meaning the true God makes a bow to an image lawful, what was wrong with the calf?" If nothing, Scripture is contradicted; if something, intent doesn't make the bow lawful.

check: If he says something was wrong with the calf, what follows?

expect: Intent, meaning the true God, doesn't make the bow to an image lawful.

## tactic-just-the-facts

say: Just the Facts catches a claim built on wrong facts: ask what exactly the claim is, and whether it's accurate. It's called for when he rests on a wrong fact, such as saying statues are only reminders, like a nativity set. Ask, "Does your church only look at them?", since Trent itself speaks of "the images which we kiss, and before which we uncover our head and bow down."

check: When he says statues are only reminders, what fact do you set beside it?

expect: What his church actually decreed: Trent's kiss and bow (or Nicaea II's kiss, bow, incense and lights). A reminder is only looked at.

## their-answers-1

say: The most common answer is, we venerate, we don't worship; proskynesis isn't latreia. Agree that they differ, then show that the commandment forbids both: "Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them," where bow down is proskynēseis and serve is latreusēs.

check: How do you reply to "we venerate, we don't worship"?

expect: Agree that they differ, and show the commandment forbids both the bow (proskynesis) and the service (latreia).

## their-answers-2

say: He may say the English word worship just means honor, as in "your worship." The question isn't about English: in the Greek the Orthodox read, the commandment forbids proskynesis to images, and in Catholic Bibles it forbids bowing down.

check: What do you answer when he says the English word worship just means honor?

expect: The question isn't about English; their own Bibles forbid the bow (proskynesis; bowing down) to images.

## their-answers-3

say: He may say the honor passes to the prototype, citing Basil, Trent or the Catechism. The pagans said the same, and Israel aimed the calf's feast at the LORD, yet God still said, "they have worshipped it." And Basil wrote of the Son as the Father's living image, not of painted boards.

check: What did God say of the calf, though Israel aimed the feast at the LORD?

expect: "They have worshipped it"; the honor did not pass on to the LORD in God's judgment.

## their-answers-4

say: He may say the Hebrew word means an idol, the image of a false god, so his images aren't among those the commandment means. Scripture marks the thing by what is made and what is done to it: Moses forbids the graven image because Israel saw no form of the LORD, the calf stood for the LORD, and the law forbids any image set up "to bow down unto it." An image becomes one of them when it is bowed to.

check: What makes an image one of those the commandment forbids?

expect: Being bowed to; Scripture marks it by what is done to it, not by which god is meant.

## their-answers-5

say: He may say God commanded the cherubim and the serpent. Yes, and he never commanded anyone to bow to them. The cherubim were behind the veil, where the people never saw them, and the serpent was looked at for healing, and broken once it was censed.

check: Did God ever command anyone to bow to the cherubim or the serpent?

expect: No; the cherubim were hidden behind the veil, and the serpent was broken once it was censed.

## their-answers-6

say: He may say the serpent was destroyed only because Israel made it a god beside Baal. The text gives one reason: "the children of Israel did burn incense to it." It doesn't say they called it a god, and Hezekiah's name for it, Nehushtan, says what it was: brass.

check: What one reason does the text give for breaking the serpent?

expect: Israel burned incense to it.

## their-answers-7

say: He may point to the ark: Joshua fell before it, a psalm says to worship at his footstool, and God made Dagon fall before it and struck those who dishonored it. But the ark was the LORD's throne, "which dwelleth between the cherubims," not a likeness of anyone, and the bow was to God present there.

check: Why is the ark no pattern for bowing to an image?

expect: It was God's throne, not a likeness of anyone; the bow was to God present there.

## their-answers-8

say: The ark's history runs against icons too. It was covered whenever it moved, and to look into it or touch it was death, while icons are set out to be seen, touched and kissed.

check: How was the ark treated differently from icons?

expect: It was covered when moved, and looking into it or touching it brought death; icons are set out to be seen, touched and kissed.

## their-answers-9

say: He may say Jacob venerated the top of Joseph's staff, from Hebrews eleven, verse twenty-one. Jacob "worshipped, leaning upon the top of his staff," and the Hebrew of Genesis forty-seven has him bow "upon the bed's head," as David later bowed in prayer on his bed. Either way he worships God upon the staff or bed; no text says he worshipped the staff.

check: According to the text, whom did Jacob worship?

expect: God, leaning on the staff (or on the bed's head); no text says he worshipped the staff.

## their-answers-10

say: He may say, with John of Damascus, that God has now been seen in Christ, so he can be depicted. God had been seen in form before Sinai, yet the ban stood, and the commandment forbids bowing to images of "any thing… in the earth," Christ's human body included. After the incarnation the apostles still forbid idols and never command an image.

check: If God was seen in form before Sinai, what happened to the ban?

expect: It stood anyway; seeing God did not lift it.

## their-answers-11

say: He may say Christ is the image of God, and the Greek word is icon. Christ is the living image, and we see his glory by faith in the gospel, not in paint.

check: Where do we see the glory of Christ, the living image?

expect: By faith in the gospel, not in paint.

## their-answers-12

say: He may say it's like kissing a photo of his mother. No one sets incense before her photo or is cursed for not kissing it, and Scripture names kissing as idolatrous homage.

check: What does the council do for icons that no one does for a family photo?

expect: Sets incense before them and curses those who won't kiss them.

## their-answers-13

say: He may say bowing isn't always worship: Jacob bowed to Esau, Israel bowed to the LORD and the king, and Christ makes his enemies bow at the church's feet. Agreed: a bow to a living man can be civil honor, and the commandment doesn't forbid it. It forbids the bow to images, and none of these bows is to an image; and when a bow of devotion was given to a living apostle or an angel, he refused it.

check: What do all his examples of bowing lack?

expect: None of them is a bow to an image; the commandment forbids the bow to images.

## their-answers-14

say: He may say you can't judge the heart, since man looks on the outward appearance. Grant the heart. But the commandment governs the body, "bow down," and God judged the calf by the act, though the feast was "to the LORD."

check: What part of a person does the commandment govern?

expect: The body: "bow down"; and God judged the calf by the act.

## their-answers-15

say: He may say God worked through relics, Paul's handkerchiefs and Elisha's bones. God healed by them, but no one is told to bow to them, kiss them or cense them, and they are not images; God buried Moses himself, and "no man knoweth of his sepulchre." Then return to the bow.

check: Is anyone told to bow to the relics God healed by?

expect: No; and they are not images. Return to the bow.

## their-answers-16

say: He may say, as Jonathan Pageau does, that we magnify God's name without worshipping the name as God, and icons are the same. God commanded praise of his name, but at Sinai he gave words and withheld a form, "ye heard the voice of the words, but saw no similitude," and forbade the image for that reason. A name is not a likeness made by hands.

check: What is the difference between God's name and an icon?

expect: God commanded praise of his name, but a name is not a likeness made by hands; he withheld a form and forbade the image.

## their-answers-17

say: He may say you wouldn't trample or spit on a picture of Christ, so you already honor images. Refusing to insult a picture is not bowing to it: I wouldn't trample my mother's photo, and I don't cense it either. When God's servants were ordered to bow to an image or die, they would not bow.

check: Is refusing to insult a picture the same as bowing to it?

expect: No; and God's servants refused to bow to an image even on pain of death.

## their-answers-18

say: He may say that what you do to the image you do to the one it shows, as with "the least of these," since man is God's image. Yes: the image God made is living man, and we honor God by doing good to him. That is no warrant for bowing to boards, and when living men, God's own image, were given a bow of devotion, they refused it.

check: What did living men do when they were given a bow of devotion?

expect: They refused it (Peter; Paul and Barnabas).

## their-answers-19

say: He may say, with the Seventh Council, that the commandment was only against the gods of Canaan. It covers images of things in heaven too, and God judged Israel's images of himself.

check: Whose images did God judge in Israel, besides those of Canaan's gods?

expect: Israel's images of the LORD himself, such as the calf.

## their-answers-20

say: He may say the early church had images, in the catacombs and at Dura-Europos. Pictures on a wall are not bowing to them. The early apologists rejected the religious use of images, and even Basil and Gregory published Origen's rejection of it.

check: Do pictures on a wall show that the early church bowed to images?

expect: No; and the early apologists rejected the religious use of images.

## their-answers-21

say: He may say the Seventh Council settled this. The council claimed to agree with Scripture, so answer, "Let's test that at the text it cites."

check: How do you answer when he says the council settled it?

expect: The council claimed to agree with Scripture, so test that at the text it cites; don't argue its authority.

## their-answers-22

say: He may say you have crosses, nativity sets and Bible films too. Having an image isn't the sin; bowing to it is, and if we bowed to ours, the same commandment would judge us.

check: What would make our own crosses and pictures sinful?

expect: Bowing to them.

## their-answers-23

say: A Catholic may say Aquinas meant a relative worship that passes through the image. That is the prototype defense under another name, and it grants more than "we only venerate": the image of Christ is given latria. Israel's worship passed through the calf to the LORD, and God called it worship of the calf.

check: What does "relative worship" grant that "we only venerate" does not?

expect: That the image of Christ is given latria, worship.

## gospel-1

say: The whole gospel is who Jesus is, our sin, his death for sinners and his rising, and the call to repent and believe. Tell it in your own words, and let whatever you say of it be true and leave no false picture.

check: What are the parts of the whole gospel?

expect: Who Jesus is; our sin; his death for sinners and his rising; the call to repent and believe.

## gospel-2

say: Many Orthodox Christians and Catholics are looking for reverence and for Christ made near. The gospel gives both: the true image of God, seen by faith, and open access to the Father through him alone.

check: What two things does the gospel give to those looking for reverence and for Christ made near?

expect: The true image of God, seen by faith, and open access to the Father through Christ alone.

## gospel-3

say: Christ "is the image of the invisible God," Colossians one, verse fifteen, and God "hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." He is seen in the gospel, by faith: "blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed."

check: How is Christ, the true image of God, seen?

expect: In the gospel, by faith.

## gospel-4

say: "There is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus." He "ever liveth to make intercession" for those who come to God by him, so we may "come boldly unto the throne of grace."

check: Because Christ ever lives to make intercession, how may we come to God?

expect: Boldly, to the throne of grace, through Christ the one mediator.

## gospel-5

say: Before you speak of being saved, ask, "If you stood before God today, what would you trust in?" Don't ask "Are you saved?", which many Orthodox are taught to hear as arrogant.

check: What do you ask instead of "Are you saved?"

expect: "If you stood before God today, what would you trust in?"

## gospel-6

say: Then show him the gift: "He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life." And, "Being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ," "not of works, lest any man should boast."

check: According to these verses, how do we have peace with God?

expect: By faith in Christ, justified by faith, not of works.

## bridge-1

say: Exposing the flaw is not the end. Let it raise the question the gospel answers, and cross at once, as Philip began at the eunuch's own passage "and preached unto him Jesus."

check: What do you do once the flaw is exposed?

expect: Cross to Christ at once, letting the flaw raise the question the gospel answers.

## bridge-2

say: If he gives the costly answer, ask, "If God forbids the bow to images, where would you look to see Christ? May I show you where Scripture says we see him?"

check: What do you ask if he concedes?

expect: If God forbids the bow to images, where would you look to see Christ? May I show you where Scripture says we see him?

## bridge-3

say: If he dodges, don't press the point again. Park it, and give the reason for your hope: "Can I tell you why this matters so much to me?" Then tell the gospel as what Christ has done for you.

check: If he dodges the question, what do you do?

expect: Don't press it; park it, and give the reason for your hope, telling the gospel as what Christ has done for you.

## bridge-4

say: Fit the telling to the room he gives you. When he asks, or says go on, tell all of the gospel; when he has no time or will for more, tell the part this moment opens, and leave the door open with a Gospel of John, an offer to read it together, or a time to talk again.

check: What do you do when he has no time or will for more?

expect: Tell the part of the gospel the moment opens, and leave the door open (a Gospel of John, an offer to read together, a time to talk again).

## bridge-5

say: Ask, don't announce. After an argument about images, a statement of where we see Christ sounds like a contrast with his icons, but an invitation to read does not, such as, "Could we read together what the men who touched him wrote?"

check: Why offer an invitation to read rather than a statement about where we see Christ?

expect: After an argument about images, a statement reads as a contrast with his icons; an invitation keeps it a conversation.

## bridge-6

say: Leave him the question he must answer before God: "If Christ himself is the image of God, and he invites you to come straight to him, what do you need the image for?"

check: What question do you leave him to answer before God?

expect: If Christ himself is the image of God and invites you to come straight to him, what do you need the image for?

## bridge-7

say: Keep at it in love. Leave a Gospel of John, pray for him by name, and expect many conversations, for God gives the growth.

check: What do you leave with him?

expect: A Gospel of John; also pray for him by name and expect many conversations.
