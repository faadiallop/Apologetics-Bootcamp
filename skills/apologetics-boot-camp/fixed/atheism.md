# Atheists (Borrowed Ground)

Status: draft

Source: skill stamp 8afceae0

## opening-beginner

Before we begin, take a moment to pray.

Here is how this training works. We ask questions instead of making speeches, so that what the atheist already uses does the work. Christ silenced his opponents with questions, and Greg Koukl's book Tactics trains the same skill.

The argument is called Borrowed Ground. In one sentence: to argue against God, the atheist must stand on things only God provides, logic that holds everywhere, a mind that can be trusted, a nature that stays orderly, and right and wrong that truly bind, so he is standing on God's ground to deny him.

Why this approach? Arguments from evidence, such as the universe's beginning, design or the resurrection, often stall with an atheist, because he judges each piece by a standard of evidence that has already ruled God out. The real dispute is over that standard. So we don't ask him to accept anything new. We ask about four things he already uses every time he argues, and show that his view can't account for them. That is Scripture's own account of him: he knows God and pushes that knowledge away.

Here is what will happen. First come short lessons, each checked with one question. Then come practice conversations in which I play an atheist, with quick coaching after each. Most of the practice goes to the parts of the method you find hardest, taken in the order Koukl teaches them. Once the method is sure, we'll practice the gospel and how to cross to it. Then come real conversations, and at the end you'll teach someone else.

Would you like to start with some background on who atheists are, just the facts that bear on this approach? Or shall we go straight to the first lesson?

## background-1

say: About three in ten American adults have no religion. Of these, about one in six calls himself an atheist and one in five an agnostic, and most say "nothing in particular." So about one American in twenty is a self-described atheist; most of them are men, and most are under fifty.

check: About how many Americans call themselves atheists?

expect: About one in twenty (about one in six of the roughly three in ten with no religion). The gist of the figure is enough.

## background-2

say: Atheists make two kinds of claim. Some say there is no God, but many today say only that they "lack belief" in God, so that the whole burden of proof falls on the believer. Antony Flew made that definition popular in 1972, and Flew himself later came to believe in a God.

check: What are the two kinds of claim atheists make?

expect: That there is no God; or only that they lack belief in God, which puts the whole burden of proof on the believer.

expect: The New Atheists (Dawkins, Hitchens, Harris, Dennett), and online Matt Dillahunty and The Atheist Experience. Any of these names, or "the New Atheists," is enough.

## foundation-1

## foundation-3

It says that "when they knew God, they glorified him not as God," and that they "hold the truth in unrighteousness."

## foundation-4

Calvin taught that a sense of God is engraved on every mind, so that no one can plead ignorance, and Aquinas held that knowing in a general way that God exists is implanted in us by nature.

check: According to Psalm nineteen, how far does the heavens' voice reach?

expect: Everywhere: there is no speech or language where it is not heard; their line is gone out through all the earth. So every man has the knowledge of God (Calvin, Aquinas).

## foundation-5

say: So there is no neutral ground: we don't treat God as a hypothesis the atheist may judge by his own standard. Paul told the Athenians that "in him we live, and move, and have our being." The atheist reasons with a mind God made, in a world God orders, by laws that reflect God's own mind, and that is the borrowed ground.

check: Why don't we treat God as a hypothesis the atheist may judge by his own standard?

expect: There is no neutral ground: the atheist lives in God and reasons with a mind God made, in a world God orders, by laws that reflect God's mind. He is on God's ground, and he already knows God.

## foundation-6

## foundation-7

expect: We don't adopt his standard as if it were neutral (the first saying), but we follow his standard to its end to show it can't carry the weight he puts on it (the second).

## tactic-gather

say: The first step is to gather, by asking, "What do you mean by that?" whenever he uses a loaded word or states his view loosely, so you neither misunderstand nor misrepresent him. With an atheist, ask, "What do you mean by 'atheist'?", "What do you think is ultimately real?" and "Where do you think right and wrong come from?", then feed it back: "So, if I understand you, everything is matter and energy, and morality is about well-being?"

check: When he uses a loaded word, what do you ask?

expect: "What do you mean by that?" (in any words that ask him to explain), so as to understand him before answering, then feeding his view back to him.

## tactic-burden-of-proof

say: The second step keeps the burden of proof on whoever makes the claim: when he makes one, ask, "How did you come to that conclusion?" It makes him give reasons, not only opinions. With an atheist, ask, "What would count as evidence for God?" and then, "Is that standard of evidence itself something the senses can test?"

check: When he makes a claim, what do you ask?

expect: "How did you come to that conclusion?" or any question that asks for his reasons, because whoever makes the claim bears the burden.

## tactic-rescues-professors-ploy

say: When he shifts the burden onto you and you have made no claim, you owe no proof: "I've only asked questions so far. Your view is the one on the table." An atheist often says, "Atheism is just a lack of belief. The burden of proof is all on you." Then ask, "What do you think is ultimately real?", because his view of the world carries a burden too.

check: If he says the burden of proof is all on you, what do you ask him?

expect: What he thinks is ultimately real; his view carries a burden too, and he owes no proof for a claim he hasn't made.

## tactic-rescues-hot-seat

say: When he outclasses you, switch from persuading to fact-finding: ask him to slow down and explain his view and his reasons, take notes, and close with "Let me think about it." A philosophy student may pile on that your argument is circular, that you can't know your revelation came from God, and whether God is bound by logic. Don't bluff and don't give up; ask him to explain each one, and take it away to think over.

check: When he outclasses you, what do you switch to?

expect: Fact-finding: asking him to explain his view and reasons, taking notes, and closing with "Let me think about it," without bluffing or giving up.

## tactic-staying-on-topic

say: When he raises a side issue or slides off the question, return to the one question, such as, "Where does your view get the logic you're arguing with?" Let a passing jab pass; park a real question or hurt, honoring it in one sentence and asking to come back to it; and if he keeps drifting, narrate: "I asked where logic comes from, and we've moved to Christian wars. Could we finish that first?" If he asks about the laws in Leviticus, park it: "I'd like to come back to that. By what standard do you call it denigrating?"

check: Which way back fits a real question or a hurt?

expect: Park it: honor it in one sentence, ask to come back to it later, and return to the one question.

## tactic-steamroller

say: When he keeps interrupting or piling on, take three steps, one for each interruption. At the first, stop him, "I'm not quite finished," and ask for time to answer one challenge before the next; at the second, ask gently, "Do you want an answer, or an audience?"; at the third, give him the last word and leave, with kind words and no jab. With an online atheist firing jabs about the "presup script," you might leave with an offer to read John one together another time.

check: What do you do at his third interruption?

expect: Give him the last word and leave kindly, with no jab and the door left open.

## tactic-lead

say: The third step uses questions that make the point: once he has stated his view and his reasons, ask questions where each "yes" walks him one step toward the conclusion, so the flaw shows without your asserting it. Run one of the four lines to its end; on morality, ask, "Is it wrong to torture a child for fun, even if everyone voted for it? What makes it wrong, in a world of matter and chance?" After the question that exposes the flaw, wait in silence.

check: When do you begin these leading questions?

expect: After he has stated his view and his reasons. Each "yes" is a step, and the flaw is shown by a question, not asserted.

## tactic-turnabout

say: When he leads you with questions toward a point he hasn't stated, or makes a claim in the form of a question, don't answer the trap. Ask him to state his point, "What are you getting at?" or "Are you saying…?", and then ask his reasons for it. If he asks, "Who designed the designer?", ask what he is getting at before you answer.

check: When he leads you with a question, what do you ask him?

expect: To state his point ("What are you getting at?"), and then his reasons for it, instead of answering the trap.

## tactic-suicide

say: Some claims fail their own standard, and a question shows it. You'll hear one when he says, "What can be asserted without evidence can be dismissed without evidence." Ask, "Is that rule itself backed by evidence?"

check: What is wrong with a claim of this kind?

expect: It fails its own standard. The rule that what lacks evidence can be dismissed has no evidence itself.

## tactic-sibling-rivalry

say: Sometimes two of his claims cancel each other, and a question sets them side by side. He calls the suffering in the world evil, yet his view says the universe has "no evil and no good." Ask, "Is the suffering in the world truly evil, or only something we dislike?"

check: Which two of his claims cancel each other here?

expect: That suffering is truly evil (his problem of evil), and his view that the universe has no evil and no good.

## tactic-infanticide

say: Sometimes a claim depends on the very thing it denies. He uses logic to argue that there is only matter, yet logic isn't made of matter. Ask, "Are the laws of logic made of matter? If not, where do they fit in a world that is only matter?"

check: What does a claim of this kind depend on?

expect: The very thing it denies: his naturalism denies anything beyond matter, yet depends on logic, which isn't matter.

## tactic-taking-the-roof-off

say: Some views, followed through consistently, end in absurdity, so you follow his to its end. If his mind came from blind processes that favor survival, it can't be trusted to find truth, even about God. Ask, "If your mind came from blind processes that favor survival, not truth, why trust it to tell you there's no God?"

check: Where does his view of the mind end, when you follow it through?

expect: In absurdity: he can't trust the mind he is using, even to tell him there's no God.

## tactic-rhodes-scholar

say: Sometimes he leans on experts without giving their reasons, so you ask for the reasons. If he says almost no philosopher takes seriously the claim that objective morality requires God, a head count isn't a reason. Ask, "Which of their arguments do you find strongest?"

check: What does a head count of experts leave out?

expect: Their reasons. Ask for the experts' arguments.

## tactic-just-the-facts

say: Some claims rest on wrong facts, so you check the facts with a question. If he says you just assume the universe would be chaotic without God, that isn't what we say; we say nothing in his view promises order. Ask, "Why do you expect the future to be like the past?"

check: What do we actually say about order in his view of the world?

expect: Not that it would be chaotic, but that nothing in his view promises order.

## their-answers-1

say: Asked where logic fits in a world of only matter, he may say the laws are just conventions. Then a society could vote contradictions true, and his arguments bind no one who doesn't share his convention. Ask, "Could a culture make contradictions true by agreement? Then why must I accept your argument?"

check: If logic is only a convention, what could a society do?

expect: Vote contradictions true; and then his arguments bind no one who doesn't share his convention.

## their-answers-2

say: He may say the laws of logic only describe how the world behaves. Then he needs the order of nature to say they'll hold tomorrow, and they hold even of things no one has ever observed. Ask, "Did non-contradiction hold before anyone observed it? Will it hold tomorrow, and how do you know?"

check: If logic only describes the world, what does he need in order to say it will hold tomorrow?

expect: The order of nature, which his view can't promise. (Logic also holds of things no one has observed.)

## their-answers-3

say: He may say logic is only in our minds, or universal only within our species. Then it differs by brain, it didn't hold before minds existed, and his charge that God contradicts himself reaches no further than human brains. Ask, "Before there were brains, could a thing both be and not be?"

check: What do you ask if he says logic exists only in human minds?

expect: "Before there were brains, could a thing both be and not be?" or the same in his own words.

## their-answers-4

say: He may say the laws of logic are a brute fact, or that he doesn't know where they come from and doesn't need to. Then he has stopped explaining, the very thing he faults believers for. Ask, "Then you're trusting something you can't account for. Isn't that what you fault believers for?"

check: If he says logic just is, what has he done?

expect: Stopped explaining, which he faults believers for; he trusts something he can't account for.

## their-answers-5

say: Asked why he trusts a mind shaped for survival, he may say that true beliefs help us survive. But useful and true are not the same, as Darwin's own doubt shows. Ask, "Can a belief be useful for survival and still be false?"

check: What is wrong with saying true beliefs help us survive?

expect: Useful and true are not the same; a belief can help survival and still be false.

## their-answers-6

say: He may say science shows that reason works. But then he uses reason to vouch for reason.

check: What does he do when he says science shows reason works?

expect: He uses reason to vouch for reason.

## their-answers-7

say: Asked why he expects the future to be like the past, he may say, "It always has been." That uses the past to vouch for the past, as Hume saw, and as Russell's chicken found out.

check: What is wrong with answering, "It always has been"?

expect: It uses past experience to vouch for itself, assuming what it sets out to prove (Hume; Russell's chicken).

## their-answers-8

say: He may answer, "Science." But science depends on the order of nature.

check: Why can't science answer the question?

expect: Science itself depends on the order of nature.

## their-answers-9

say: He may say, "I just assume it," or that he has no idea why nature is uniform. Thank him for the honest answer. Then ask, "Then you trust it without a reason. Isn't that your definition of faith?"

check: When he says he just assumes it, what has he admitted?

expect: That his deepest belief is held without evidence, which is his own definition of faith.

## their-answers-10

say: He may say induction is imperfect and doesn't give a hundred percent. Agree that it isn't certain, and ask, "Why expect it to work even most of the time tomorrow?" Probability assumes the same uniformity.

check: Why doesn't probability get him out?

expect: Probability assumes the same uniformity of nature.

## their-answers-11

say: Asked whether suffering is truly evil, he may say yes. But on his view there is "no evil and no good," as Dawkins puts it, so ask where the standard comes from.

check: If he says suffering is truly evil, what do you ask?

expect: Where his standard comes from, since on his view there is no evil and no good.

## their-answers-12

say: He may say harm is the standard. But measuring harm tells how much, not why causing it is wrong. Ask, "Why ought anyone to lessen harm, if he defines morality another way?"

check: What does measuring harm fail to tell?

expect: Why causing harm is wrong.

## their-answers-13

say: He may call the question stupid, since nothing is self-justifying. Then he has granted, in his own words, that his view can't justify reason or morals, while one ground does justify itself: God, "because he could swear by no greater, he sware by himself." Ask, "Then why should someone who lacks your intuition care about well-being?"

check: What has he granted when he says nothing is self-justifying?

expect: That his view can't justify reason or morals; God is the one ground that justifies itself.

## their-answers-14

say: He may say, "I don't think I can answer that." Ask once more, "Was it evil, whatever anyone thinks?", then wait and don't answer for him, as Christ let "We cannot tell" stand. If he still won't say, leave the stone in his shoe: "If you can't say it was evil, can you still say God is wrong to allow it?"

check: If he still won't say it was evil, what do you ask?

expect: Whether, if he can't say it was evil, he can still say God is wrong to allow it. He doesn't answer for him.

## their-answers-15

say: He may say suffering is only something we dislike. Then his problem of evil has no evil to charge God with.

check: If evil is only disliked, what happens to his problem of evil?

expect: It has no evil to charge God with.

## their-answers-16

say: He may say wrong is wrong by agreement, or "intersubjective." Then it turns right when the agreement changes, so ask, "If the conversation had gone the other way, would it be right?" Scripture says, "Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil."

check: If wrong is only wrong by agreement, what happens when the agreement changes?

expect: It turns right; a society that agreed on the Holocaust would make it right.

## their-answers-17

say: He may say, "We are not debating worldviews today." Show him it is the topic: his charges against God use logic and a moral standard, and the question is where his view gets them.

check: Why is his view of the world the topic after all?

expect: His charges against God use logic and a moral standard, and the question is where his view gets them.

## their-answers-18

say: When he says atheism is just a lack of belief, remember that a rock lacks belief in God. He is doing more: calling belief irrational and God unlikely, and those are claims, which carry a burden. And Scripture says no one simply lacks the knowledge of God.

check: How is he doing more than a rock that lacks belief in God?

expect: He makes claims (belief is irrational, God is unlikely), and claims carry a burden.

## their-answers-19

say: He may say you assume God to prove God, so it's circular. But every final authority is defended by itself, as he defends reason by reasoning, and of God Scripture says, "because he could swear by no greater, he sware by himself." The question is not whether there is a circle, but which circle can account for reasoning at all, and his cannot account for the reason he uses inside it.

check: What is the real question about circles?

expect: Which circle can account for reasoning at all; every final authority is defended by itself.

## their-answers-20

say: He may ask how you know your revelation came from God, or that you're not insane. God is able to make himself known so that his people know it is he: "My sheep hear my voice." And the doubt cuts deeper against him, for if he can't rule out that he is insane, he can't trust the reasoning behind his doubt.

check: How does the doubt about insanity cut against him?

expect: If he can't rule out that he is insane, he can't trust the reasoning behind his own doubt.

## their-answers-21

say: He may ask why the Christian God, and not Zeus or a deist god. The argument needs a God both absolute, above all and unchanging, and personal, one who thinks, speaks and commands. Zeus is born inside the world, so he is finite, and a deist god is silent, so he gives no moral law and promises no order; only the God of Scripture is absolute, personal and has spoken.

check: What two things must God be, for the argument to work?

expect: Absolute and personal (and he has spoken).

## their-answers-22

say: He may ask whether something is good because God commands it, or whether God commands it because it's good. Neither: the good is not a standard above God, and it is not arbitrary, but God's own unchanging character, which his commands express. As Jesus said, "there is none good but one, that is, God."

check: If neither, what is the good?

expect: God's own unchanging character, which his commands express.

## their-answers-23

say: He may say evolution explains our reason and our morals. On his view it would explain why we have them, not why they're true or binding, and Darwin's own doubt and the claim that ethics is an illusion are the result. Don't debate evolution itself.

check: Even on his view, what can evolution not explain about reason and morals?

expect: Why they are true or binding; only why we have them.

## their-answers-24

say: He may ask, "Who made God?" Only what begins needs a maker, and God has no beginning. Ask what he means by "God," because he is often picturing a being inside the world.

check: Why doesn't God need a maker?

expect: Only what begins needs a maker, and God has no beginning.

## their-answers-25

say: He may say there's no evidence for God. Ask what would count, for everything is evidence for God, since everything is his work. And his rule that only what the senses can test is real cannot itself be tested by the senses.

check: What is wrong with the rule that only what the senses can test is real?

expect: It can't itself be tested by the senses.

## their-answers-26

say: When he asks why a good and all-powerful God allows so much suffering, first ask whether he is grieving. If he is, weep with him before you argue.

check: What do you ask first when he raises suffering?

expect: Whether he is grieving; if he is, weep with him before arguing.

## their-answers-27

say: Then, real evil needs a real standard. God works even evil for good, and in Christ he entered suffering himself and will end it.

check: What does real evil need?

expect: A real standard. (God works even evil for good; in Christ he entered suffering and will end it.)

## their-answers-28

say: He may say punishing the innocent for the guilty is immoral. No third party is punished against his will: "God was in Christ, reconciling the world unto himself," so the judge bears the cost himself, and the Son said, "I lay it down of myself." Then ask where "immoral" comes from on his view.

check: Why is the cross not a third party punished against his will?

expect: God was in Christ: the judge bears the cost himself, and the Son laid down his life of himself. Then ask where "immoral" comes from on his view.

## their-answers-29

say: He may say science explains the world, and God is just a gap-filler. But science depends on the order of nature and a trustworthy mind. We don't put God in the gaps; we say he is why there is an orderly world to study at all.

check: Why is God not a gap-filler?

expect: He is why there is an orderly world to study at all; science depends on that order and on a trustworthy mind.

## their-answers-30

say: He may say that to prove only the Christian God makes sense of the world, you'd have to check every other view, and nobody can. You don't check them one by one, because without God there are really only two kinds: in one, everything is ruled in the end by fixed laws nobody chose, which leaves no room for real change or choices; in the other, it's ruled by chance, which leaves no reason to trust any law, even logic. Only a personal God who made both the laws and the changing world holds them together.

check: Without God, what are the two kinds of view?

expect: Everything ruled by fixed laws nobody chose (no room for change or choices), or by chance (no reason to trust any law, even logic).

## their-answers-31

say: So ask which view he thinks works, and look at that one, asking whether what's behind everything in it is personal or impersonal, and whether laws nobody chose, or chance, can promise the world will keep making sense. Be patient, and don't treat this as a silver bullet. Claim no more than you can show, since Bosserman, who made this case, grants the proof falls if one alternative works on its own terms.

check: When he says you can't check every view, which one do you look at?

expect: The one he thinks works: is what's behind it personal or impersonal, and can its laws or chance promise the world keeps making sense? Claim no more than you can show.

## their-answers-32

say: He may say, "I can be good without God." Agree that atheists can do good things; the question is what makes anything good.

check: When he says he can be good without God, what is the real question?

expect: What makes anything good. (Agree that atheists can do good things.)

## gospel-1

say: The whole gospel is who Jesus is, our sin, his death for sinners and his rising, and the call to repent and believe. Tell it in your own words, true to Scripture.

check: What are the parts of the whole gospel?

expect: Who Jesus is; our sin; his death for sinners and his rising; the call to repent and believe.

## gospel-2

say: The Word behind all reason "was God… And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us." Augustine found in the philosophers that the Word was God, but not that the Word became flesh. Logic can point to the Word; only the Gospel tells of him coming among us.

check: What could Augustine not find in the philosophers?

expect: That the Word became flesh; only the gospel tells of him coming among us.

## gospel-3

say: Name the real problem, gently: it is not too little evidence but our sin, for "men loved darkness rather than light." This is true of you as well as of him.

check: What is the real problem, if not too little evidence?

expect: Our sin, his and ours alike: men loved darkness rather than light.

## gospel-4

say: Tell of the cross and the empty tomb: "Christ died for our sins… he was buried, and… he rose again the third day," and many saw him risen. God "hath given assurance unto all men, in that he hath raised him from the dead."

check: What assurance has God given all men?

expect: That he raised Jesus from the dead (after he died for our sins and was buried).

## gospel-5

say: Then the call: God "commandeth all men every where to repent," and "Be ye reconciled to God." The restless heart finds its rest in him, as Augustine said.

check: What does God command all men everywhere to do?

expect: Repent, and be reconciled to God.

## gospel-6

say: He may say lust doesn't make anyone a bad person, and no one is a liar unless he lies again and again. Ask, "How many lies make a liar?" Jesus measures the heart, "whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her hath committed adultery with her already in his heart," so say gently that this is true of you too.

check: How does Jesus measure sin?

expect: By the heart: lust in the heart is adultery already. And it is true of the speaker too.

## gospel-7

say: He may ask how a gift can be free if you must humble yourself to receive it. Faith earns nothing; it receives, as a hand takes a gift: "to him that worketh not, but believeth… his faith is counted for righteousness," and "the gift of God is eternal life."

check: How does faith receive the gift?

expect: As a hand takes a gift; faith earns nothing.

## gospel-8

say: He may object that those who won't accept the message must depart into everlasting fire. He quotes Jesus rightly, and the fire was "prepared for the devil and his angels"; and God says, "I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked." Ask, "Would a good judge let evil go unjudged?"

check: What do you ask when he objects to judgment?

expect: "Would a good judge let evil go unjudged?" (His outrage borrows the standard; God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked.)

## bridge-1

say: Exposing the flaw is not the end: it shows he stands on God's ground, and the gospel tells him who that God is and how he came looking for him. So cross at once, as Philip "began at the same scripture, and preached unto him Jesus."

check: What does the gospel tell him that the flaw cannot?

expect: Who the God is on whose ground he stands, and how he came looking for him. So cross at once.

## bridge-2

say: If he concedes, saying something like "I can't account for it," ask the bridge question: "Scripture says the reason we can think at all is that a God who thinks made us, and that we know him and push him away. May I tell you what he did about that?"

check: What do you ask once he concedes he can't account for it?

expect: The bridge question in substance: Scripture says we can think because a God who thinks made us, and we know him and push him away; may I tell you what he did about that?

## bridge-3

say: If he dodges, don't press the point again. Park it, and give the reason for the hope that is in you: "Can I tell you why this matters so much to me?" Then tell the gospel as what Christ has done for you, as Paul did before Agrippa.

check: If he dodges, what do you do instead of pressing the point?

expect: Park it, give the reason for his hope ("Can I tell you why this matters so much to me?"), and tell the gospel as what Christ has done for him.

## bridge-4

say: Cross within two turns, rather than arguing on. And don't stop at "some god exists," because the goal is Christ.

check: Why don't you stop once he grants that some god exists?

expect: Because the goal is Christ.

## bridge-5

say: Fit the telling to the room he gives you. When he asks, or says "go on," tell all of it, as Philip, Peter and Paul did when the way was opened.

check: When he says "go on," how much of the gospel do you tell?

expect: All of it.

## bridge-6

say: When he has no time or will for more, tell what this moment opens, and leave the door open: a Gospel of John, an offer to read it together, a time to talk again. Don't press the gospel after the door has closed.

check: What do you leave him when he has no time for more?

expect: An open door: a Gospel of John, an offer to read it together, or a time to talk again, without pressing the gospel.

## bridge-7

say: Ask, don't announce. "May I show you…?" keeps it a conversation.

check: Why ask leave instead of announcing?

expect: It keeps it a conversation.

## bridge-8

say: If he takes a Gospel but stays where he is, don't try to argue him into more than he gave. Thank him, and ask one thing: "Would you read John and tell me where it goes wrong?" The word does its own work, for it "shall not return unto me void."

check: What one thing do you ask a man who takes a Gospel but stays where he is?

expect: "Would you read John and tell me where it goes wrong?" without arguing for more.

## bridge-9

say: A plain "No, thanks" ends it. Don't add a warning to a stranger walking away.

check: What do you do after a stranger says, "No, thanks"?

expect: Let it end, with no warning added.

## bridge-10

say: Keep at it in love: have him to your table, as Christ ate with sinners. An invitation to a meal, to read, or to Christ himself may serve better than one to church; one former atheist said that because her friends didn't invite her to church, she knew it was safe to be friends.

check: What kinds of invitation may serve better than one to church?

expect: To a meal, to read, or to Christ himself.

## bridge-11

say: When the time is right, ask him plainly whether he will keep sitting on the fence, as Elijah asked, "How long halt ye between two opinions?" One friend's question, "Do you really want to sit on the fence forever?", moved an atheist scholar to read the Gospels.

check: What plain question can you ask him when the time is right?

expect: Whether he will keep sitting on the fence.

## bridge-12

say: Leave him a Gospel of John, offer to read it together, pray for him, and stay his friend after the argument. One plants, another waters, and God gives the growth.

check: Who gives the growth?

expect: God. Our part is to leave a Gospel, offer to read it together, pray, and stay his friend.
