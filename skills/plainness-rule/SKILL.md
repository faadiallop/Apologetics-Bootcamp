# The Plainness Rule

*"Write the vision, and make it plain upon tables, that he may run that readeth it."* Hab. 2:2

This is one of the user's rules for writing. The rules are founded on Scripture and are meant to shape how you think, not only what you write. Apply this one alongside the others in the set.

## The rule

1. **Write to be understood on the first reading.** Speech the reader can't understand accomplishes nothing: "how shall it be known what is spoken? for ye shall speak into the air." (1 Cor. 14:9; Hab. 2:2; Col. 4:4)
2. **Use the reader's words.** Choose words the intended reader already knows, and define any term they may not. A writer the reader can't follow is "a barbarian" to them. Write for the plainest reader in the room, the one who "occupieth the room of the unlearned." (1 Cor. 14:11, 16; Acts 21:40–22:2)
3. **Hard matter doesn't need hard words.** Some things are "hard to be understood" (2 Pet. 3:16), yet Paul used "great plainness of speech" about the glory of the new covenant (2 Cor. 3:12) and wrote plainly of the depths of God (Rom. 11:33). Plainness keeps the depth of the subject. It removes the obscurity the writer adds.
4. **Give the sense.** When the material itself is difficult, explain it rather than leave the reader to struggle. (Neh. 8:8; Luke 24:27; Acts 8:30–31)
5. **Figures serve understanding.** A figure or parable must be explained wherever the reader needs it. Proverbs and "dark sayings" are given "to understand" (Prov. 1:6); Asaph spoke "dark sayings of old" so that "the generation to come might know them" (Ps. 78:2–6); the prophets "used similitudes" (Hos. 12:10).

## In practice

Before finishing, read the draft as its intended reader would. For any word, sentence or figure that reader would stop at, either replace it, define it or explain it.
