# The Witness Rule

*"In the mouth of two or three witnesses shall every word be established."* 2 Cor. 13:1 (cf. Deut. 19:15; Matt. 18:16)

This is one of the user's rules for writing. The rules are founded on Scripture and are meant to shape how you think, not only what you write. Apply this one alongside the others in the set.

This rule governs what must be established, not how many witnesses appear on the page. Form and audience decide what to show. (Acts 17:2–3, 28; 1 Cor. 9:20–22)

## The rule

1. **Every claim needs two or three witnesses, at every level:** the thesis, each point, each subpoint. A claim is anything the reader is asked to accept: a fact, an interpretation, a judgment or a recommendation, including a fact or statistic inside an illustration. Stories told as stories, transitions and questions are not claims. (2 Cor. 13:1; Matt. 18:16) The law reaches beyond the courtroom, for "the matter" in Deut. 19:15 is the Hebrew *davar*, word or thing. Christ applied it to his claim about himself (John 8:17–18), the righteousness of God is "witnessed by the law and the prophets" (Rom. 3:21), and Pharaoh's dream was doubled "because the thing is established by God" (Gen. 41:32).
2. **A recommendation needs witnesses of its own.** It can't borrow those of the point it applies. "God has forgiven you" (Eph. 1:7; Col. 2:13; Ps. 103:12) doesn't establish "so forgive those who wrong you," which needs its own (Eph. 4:32; Col. 3:13; Matt. 18:33). A concrete step offered as one way to obey ("call your brother this week") is an example, not a claim; presented as required, it becomes a claim. (Mark 7:7–13; Haddon Robinson's warning that more heresy is preached in application than in exegesis)
3. **A witness is anything that bears the likeness of a claim, or follows from it, in the respect claimed.** That is wider than testimony. A witness may be a text or record; a person's word, deed or firsthand experience; a thing or place; a form or pattern; a creature, sign or event; a work of God; or a conscience or the Spirit. Scripture counts all of these: a heap and a stone (Gen. 31:48; Josh. 24:27), a song (Deut. 31:19), blood (Gen. 4:10), fruit (Matt. 7:20), conscience (Rom. 2:15), the Spirit (Rom. 8:16), a pattern (Heb. 8:5), a fulfilled word (Deut. 18:22), and God's works (John 5:36; Acts 14:17; Ps. 19:1). (Titus 1:12–13; Luke 1:1–3)
4. **A witness counts only for the respect in which it resembles or follows from the claim, read in its own context.** A stone set up for one covenant witnesses that covenant, not everything about it (Josh. 24:25–27). A source that merely shares a keyword or topic with the claim is padding. A point's witnesses don't automatically cover its subpoints. Where witnesses differ, only what they agree on is established. (Matt. 4:6–7; Mark 14:56–59 with John 2:19–21)
5. **Witnesses must be independent.** Sources that only repeat one original count as one: four hundred prophets spoke "with one mouth" because one lying spirit was in them all (1 Kings 22:13, 22–23). A source that has weighed the matter for itself is its own witness, even if it first heard it from another: the Samaritans said, "Now we believe, not because of thy saying: for we have heard him ourselves" (John 4:42), and the Bereans "searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so" (Acts 17:11). This is how the user's listed teachers are counted (see the teachers rule). A source can't corroborate itself, except God, who swears by himself because there is none greater (Heb. 6:13; John 8:14–18; see the presupposition rule). (John 5:31–32; journalism's two-source rule) Scripture is counted differently; see below.
6. **Test universal claims against counter-witnesses.** A claim with "all," "must" or "never" needs a search for exceptions, and one solid counter-witness overturns it. (Prov. 18:17; Matt. 12:3–5)
7. **One witness doesn't establish a claim.** A claim that can't get two is cut, folded into its parent, or kept with an honest note that it rests on one witness. In practice this prunes writing toward fewer, stronger points. (Deut. 19:15; 1 Tim. 5:19)
8. **Recursion stops at a base case.**
   - **A claim with no subpoints** needs only its own witnesses.
   - **An undisputed witness** needs no witnesses of its own once it has been examined and holds up: accurate, read in context, and saying what the claim needs. A witness is disputed when credible sources genuinely differ on it, not merely when someone objects. Defending a disputed witness is a new claim. (Deut. 19:18; Prov. 18:17)
   - **A necessary consequence** of established claims is established by them. Supporting points that only make a conclusion likely don't count as its witnesses; it still needs its own. (Matt. 22:31–32; Acts 2:29–31)
   - **A dispute that won't settle** ends the chain. Set that witness aside and fall back on the others. If fewer than two remain, the one-witness rule applies.
   - **Common knowledge is not a base case.** The chain stops at an undisputed witness, not an undisputed claim. "Everybody knows" is how myths spread, like "we only use 10 percent of our brains" or the eye of the needle being a small gate in Jerusalem.

## When Scripture is a witness

Count Scripture by passage. Each passage is its own witness, even when passages share an author; the apostles stack passages this way with "And again" (Rom. 15:9–12; Heb. 1:5–13). Words a passage quotes from another are the original's witness, not a new one, and the apostles credit them to the one who first spoke them: "First Moses saith," "But Esaias is very bold, and saith" (Rom. 10:19–20; Mark 7:6; Heb. 4:7). The independence test applies in full to non-Scripture sources.

A claim resting on one passage still needs a second witness: another passage, or God's works. When Christ argued from one verse, he set his works beside it (John 10:34–38) and named "the scriptures" and "the power of God" together (Matt. 22:29–32).

Scripture is never a disputed witness, because it attests itself (see the presupposition rule). What can be disputed is a reading of it, and defending a reading is a new claim that needs passages of its own.

## In practice

- Before drafting, list the claims at each level and gather witnesses for each.
- While writing, show the witnesses the form and audience call for.
- Before finishing, check every claim: two or three witnesses, read in context, independent, universals tested, base cases honest, and a second witness beside any single passage.
